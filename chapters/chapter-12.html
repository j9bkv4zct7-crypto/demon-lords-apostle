Chapter 12

Twenty-Eight Loops

3,220 words · about 14 min

‹ Chapter 11 Contents Chapter 13 ›

"How long before you must go back to Lady Sereth, my lord?" Skeev asked as we climbed out of the Hollow, walking fast to keep up.

My clusters had been counting without my asking. "About two hours."

"Then where does my lord wish to go?" He said it with the hopeful air of someone praying the answer didn't involve any more artifacts capable of eating his master alive.

"My room. I'm going to meditate."

The relief on his face was enormous.

The castle had sunk into its deep hours: fewer patrols, fewer voices, our footsteps the only sound in the long empty corridors. A faint trace of Thalia's incense still clung to the stone of my quarters. Skeev took up his post at the door. "Skeev will stand watch. Two hours. Skeev will come for you when it is time."

I sat cross-legged on the floor and let the coat loosen and thin into a resting layer. The pocket at my hip stayed sealed, the pearl inside it silent.

Then I went inward and didn't build anything. I wasn't expanding. I was only tightening. I examined every cluster and measured every loop. I mapped and catalogued every node. The first layer cycled through me like a second heartbeat, each pass a fraction stronger than the last, and with every minute I spent attending to it, it settled more firmly, like gears finding their teeth.

Time stretched and compressed the way it does in deep focus.

"My lord." A knock on the iron. "It is time."

The pale light of Sereth's diagnostic crystals spilled into the corridor. She was bent over a semicircle of instruments, and her black eyes rose as I came in and studied me for a long moment.

"You meditated." A crystal flared under her fingers and threw a three-dimensional map of my architecture into the air between us. "Your loops are cleaner than when you left. Tighter tolerances. You self-corrected drift at three of fourteen anchors." She tilted her head. "Good. You will need every fraction of stability you have. Sit. I will not lie to you, Apostle." The black eyes held mine. "This one will make the first feel like a warmth."

"Okay."

I sat on the cracked slab. She circled me and woke the crystals one by one, blue, then white, then a deep pulsing violet, until my whole architecture hung around me like a cage of light. Every cluster, every loop, and all fourteen anchors glowed like stars in a skeletal constellation.

"The first layer put one recursive loop in each anchor. Each loop compounds by feeding its own exhaust back into itself. Simple. Elegant. Survivable." She stopped in front of me and laid a cold hand on my chest. It was like touching frozen glass. "The second nests a loop inside each existing loop. Fourteen become twenty-eight. Each child feeds on its parent's output and generates its own compounding wave at the same time. The interference between parent and child will spike harmonic resonance through your entire nervous system. In simple terms, every nerve in your body will feel as though it is being unmade and rebuilt at once, continuously, for the whole encoding." She withdrew her hand. "The first took eleven minutes. This will take about thirty. If you lose consciousness, it fails and we begin again. If your clusters reject the nested loops, the feedback eats through your anchors, and I will have to sever them by hand before they rupture." She looked over her shoulder. "Ready?"

In the doorway, Skeev was wringing his small green hands.

I closed my eyes and pulled everything into razor focus. The meditation hadn't been wasted. My mind was a blade.

"Begin."

The crystals ignited together. The hum became a tone, the tone became a frequency, the frequency locked onto my anchors, and then...

It hit.

There was no gradual wave of heat this time. It was immediate and total. Every nerve fired at once as if my skeleton had been replaced with molten wire. The nested loops burrowed into the existing architecture and threaded themselves inside their parents like parasites made of pure mathematics. My jaw locked. My fingers dug into the slab, and the stone cracked outward from my grip.

"First anchor pair encoding," Sereth said, distant and clinical and untouched. "Hold."

The exoskeleton rippled violently across my body, its crystal structure destabilizing and reforming and destabilizing again. Every fraction of a second brought a new equation and a new spike tearing through me.

Five minutes. My vision whited out, came back, whited out. My blood burned. Its temperature was spiking as the nested loops produced output with nowhere to go yet, building and pressurizing.

"Sixth anchor. Rejection rate four percent. Acceptable. Do not lose focus."

Ten minutes. A sound came out of my throat. It wasn't a scream. It was something lower and involuntary. The exoskeleton flared out in jagged spikes that crystallized and shattered in quick succession as my body tried to shed the excess.

From the doorway, Skeev whimpered.

Fifteen minutes. Halfway. Parent and child began to find each other. The interference shifted from chaos to rhythm. It was still agony, but it had structure now, and my clusters seized on the pattern and started predicting the spikes, bracing for them.

"Tenth anchor. Four remaining." Her eyes were fixed on something in the array. "Harmonic resonance is exceeding projections by eleven percent. Your architecture is absorbing more than I anticipated."

Twenty minutes. The pain changed character. It stopped feeling like destruction and started feeling like growth, like bones breaking and resetting stronger, again and again. My channels didn't widen gradually. They were torn open and rebuilt in the same heartbeat. My regeneration was climbing, and climbing fast.

Twenty-five minutes.

"Thirteenth." Something new had come into her voice. It wasn't concern. It was fascination. "One remaining."

The base of my skull, the most sensitive point in the whole architecture. When the nested loop threaded into it, my body seized rigid. Every muscle contracted at once, and the exoskeleton froze mid-ripple in a jagged corona of black crystal shards around me like a shattered halo.

Thirty minutes.

The encoding completed.

Everything released at once. Every crystal in the room exploded into luminous dust that hung in the air like frozen rain. My body unlocked, and I slumped forward over the broken slab, catching myself on both hands, breath coming in ragged pulls. Slowly the shards drew back, and the coat and pauldrons smoothed into place, trembling and settling.

Then my clusters reported.

There were twenty-eight recursive loops, all active and all synchronized, parent and child feeding each other in perfect rhythm. My output hadn't doubled. It had been squared. Every heartbeat compounded on the last, and the compounding compounded on itself.

Sereth stood motionless in the dust of her instruments.

"Cluster rejection rate," she whispered. "Zero percent." She stared at the flickering ghost of my architecture in the settling dust. "That should not be possible."

Skeev was sitting on the floor of the doorway with his eyes closed. At some point he'd fainted.

I called up the window, and the numbers had moved. The level read forty-three; somewhere between the anchors and this, the counter had ticked over. The mana pool read fourteen thousand two hundred. The rest I didn't need to read. The difference between the man who'd walked into Harren and the one slumped on this slab wasn't on the page. The numbers described only what my body could hold steady, and the loops could multiply far past it.

Below it all, one new line:

Active debuff: Anchor stabilization. No combat or high-output exertion until dawn.

"The second layer needs six hours to seat," Sereth said without looking up from gathering crystal fragments. "Nothing strenuous until dawn." She paused. "After that, I am genuinely curious what you are capable of."

Skeev stirred and blinked awake. "Did... did Skeev miss something, my lord?"

I held out my right palm and thought of the sphere.

The dark matter came faster than ever before, much faster. Mana flowed through the nested loops like water through a broken dam, each loop feeding the next, compounding and squaring. The sphere that formed above my hand was the size of a human head, and its spin was violent. Black lightning arced and snapped across its surface. Light bent visibly inward. The temperature of the room dropped sharply, and the drifting crystal dust spiraled toward the sphere and disintegrated on contact.

Sereth straightened, and for the first time since I'd known her, her eyes truly widened.

"That is not a training sphere," she said quietly. Her form flickered. "That is a weapon."

The walls groaned. Hairline cracks crept across the ceiling. Skeev, barely back on his feet, dropped flat against the doorframe. "M-my lord... the walls..."

I felt it too. The sphere was drawing more than I'd told it to, because the loops were feeding it eagerly. Too eagerly. Every heartbeat made it denser and heavier and darker. My clusters raised a flag. It wasn't danger, not yet, but the gap between what I intended and what came out had widened.

My power had grown faster than my control.

I closed my fist.

The sphere imploded into my palm with a sharp crack of displaced air, and a pressure wave rolled out through the room. Sereth's robes billowed, and Skeev's ears flattened. When I opened my hand, the palm was unmarked.

Sereth let out a slow breath. I hadn't known a wraith-kin could make that sound.

"Recalibrate your control to match your output," she said. The clinical tone was back, but something sat beneath it now, between awe and concern. "If you had lost your grip on that for a fraction of a second, it would have killed everything in this room." The black eyes held mine. "Practice before dawn. The power is there, Apostle. The precision is not. Not yet."

I rose from the ruined slab and bowed my head to her. I meant it.

"Thank you, Sereth. I'll be back with more ideas and more experiments."

Something in her face softened, by perhaps a fraction.

"I will be here," she said simply. "Bring me something interesting."

"My lord," Skeev said carefully as we crossed the outer gate, "Lady Sereth said no combat until dawn. And the woods have creatures in..."

I looked down at him.

He swallowed. "Training. Of course. Not combat. Training. Very different. Yes."

About two miles east of the castle walls, a dark treeline rose like a wall of shadow. Skeev pointed with a trembling finger. "The Ashwood, my lord. Castle grounds. Lower creatures, mostly. Shadowhares, thornbacks, the odd razorwing. Nothing above level ten." A pause. "Good for... recalibrating?"

The night air hit me, and the coat tightened against the cold. The Ashwood swallowed us: massive black-barked trees, ash-grey leaves that rustled without wind, and clusters of pale blue and sickly green fungi on the trunks giving just enough light to see by. The air smelled of char and damp soil. I stopped in a clearing thirty feet across, where the canopy filtered the red sky into thin bleeding shafts.

I raised my hand slowly this time and fed my palm a trickle, a fraction of what I'd used in the medical wing. A sphere formed, the size of an apple, and spun gently.

The loops wanted to feed it. The urge to compound was constant, every heartbeat pushing more mana toward the construct. I held them back and throttled the flow, and the sphere trembled, fighting to grow, while I kept it pinned.

Ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty. It steadied.

That was control. But it was control that lived entirely in my concentration, and concentration can lapse. Twenty-eight loops with no ceiling and only my conscious mind as the brake was a design flaw.

I needed architecture that would do the braking for me.

I didn't create new clusters. I partitioned existing ones, splitting a thin sliver from each anchor and rewriting its function from amplification to regulation. The first limiter seated at the right radius, between the loop and the output channel, like a valve in a pipe. Its rules defined themselves: passive output held to one part in a hundred of what the loops could give, unless I overrode it. It read my intent in real time. A calm thought meant calm output. An aggressive thought opened the valve. Killing intent removed the valve entirely.

The sphere stopped trembling at once. It sat in my palm perfectly still, contained and obedient, while behind the limiter the loops went on compounding. They were banking power now instead of spending it.

Then the left radius. Then the third, the fourth, the fifth.

Skeev watched from behind a thick trunk at the edge of the clearing. He didn't speak or move, and he was barely breathing.

From the sixth to the tenth, the pressure coming off my skin dropped noticeably with each installation. The fungi on the nearby trunks stopped flickering erratically and settled into their natural glow. The eleventh, twelfth, and thirteenth followed. The last went in at the base of my skull, the same anchor that had locked my body rigid an hour earlier. I threaded it in with surgical care, mapping every nerve path and every junction, and it clicked into place like the final tumbler of a lock.

Fourteen limiters, one per anchor, all reading my intent.

I thought bigger, and the sphere swelled to the size of a melon. I thought smaller, and it shrank to a marble. I thought weapon, and the valves opened, and the sphere detonated in my palm, the blast contained by the exoskeleton and absorbed before it ever reached the air.

I thought calm.

Everything stopped. The sphere dissolved, and my output dropped to baseline. The loops kept cycling behind the limiters in silence, coiling like a spring.

For the first time since the second layer, I wasn't leaking power into the world. The trees were still. The air had stopped warping around me.

Skeev's head came out from behind his tree, and he blinked twice.

"My lord," he whispered. "You feel... normal."

He was right. To anyone sensing me from outside, I'd read as unremarkable. The loops, the reserves, and the squared potential were all hidden behind fourteen governors that showed the world nothing until I decided otherwise.

I formed a marble-sized sphere.

Kill.

All fourteen limiters opened together. The sphere exploded outward, black and crackling, pulling the light inward, and the trees groaned around us.

Calm.

The limiters closed. The sphere was gone.

It was instant and seamless, like breathing.

Next, I wanted three modes, three identities the exoskeleton could become on command.

War Mode. The coat thinned and compressed into sleek, angular plates built for mobility. The pauldrons sharpened into bladed ridges, and articulated greaves locked around my legs for explosive movement. Then I reached into the anchors across my back, both scapulae and the cervical, and split their channels twelve ways, and twelve tendrils burst from my shoulder blades and upper back. Each was six feet long and segmented, tapering to a curved scythe blade, and they fanned out behind me like the legs of some nightmare insect.

They weren't like the six from the Glade. I gave each one its own sliver of processing: independent threat detection, independent targeting, independent strikes. They were twelve minds working with mine, none of them waiting for me to direct it. They tested their range together. Two planted themselves in the earth behind me like anchors. Two curled over my shoulders in a screen. The other eight fanned outward, their tips weaving slow figure-eights through the air, sensing, waiting.

I took one step, and two of them drove into the ground and launched me. I crossed thirty feet of clearing in a blink and landed on the greaves, while the tendrils reconfigured in midair: four sweeping my landing zone clear, two trailing for balance, the rest cycling through guard positions. It was brutally fast. Running had turned into something closer to teleportation.

Calm. The tendrils withdrew and the plates softened back into the coat. Skeev was pressed flat against his tree with both hands over his mouth.

Defense Mode. This time everything went the other way. The coat thickened, layer stacking on compressed layer across my chest, shoulders, arms, and legs. The pauldrons widened into broad curved shields, and massive reinforced vambraces formed for blocking and deflection. My silhouette doubled. It wasn't passive, though. I wrote counterattack protocols into the surface itself. Strike it hard enough, and the point of impact would hold the energy for a heartbeat, then detonate outward and send the attacker's own force back at him, with the loops adding interest.

I tested it by throwing a fist-sized sphere at my own chest.

It struck the vambrace dead center, and the armor drank the impact, held it, and answered. The counter-burst ripped across the clearing in a tight cone and sheared three ashwood trees off at the trunk. They toppled slowly, groaning and splintering into the undergrowth.

There wasn't a scratch on the armor.

Calm. Skeev whimpered.

Chaos Mode. I didn't build anything for this one. I simply defined it. Chaos Mode was the absence of configuration: every limiter unlocked, every loop unleashed, every compounding wave released at once with no throttle and no ceiling. It used War Mode's frame without governors, the twelve scythes running at full autonomy.

I didn't test it. My clusters ran the projection for full activation at my current level and returned a very clear result.

It would flatten the Ashwood.

I opened my eyes. Three modes, encoded and ready.

"My lord," Skeev breathed. "What are you?"

I grinned. "I'm the most badass demon in existence." A beat. "Second to the Demon Lord, that is."

Skeev stared at me. Then, slowly, his terrified expression cracked, and a grin split his face, all small sharp teeth.

"Second," he repeated, nodding vigorously. "Yes. Second. For now."

He heard himself. His eyes went huge, and both hands flew to his mouth. "Skeev did not... Skeev did not mean... the Demon Lord is eternal and supreme, and Skeev would never suggest..."

I waved it off. He let out a breath so big his whole body deflated.

Something rustled in the treeline to my left. Low to the ground, between the black trunks, two pale points of light appeared. Then another pair. Then a third.

Skeev froze. "Thornbacks," he whispered. "The mana drew them. Level six. Pack hunters."

The eyes multiplied to seven pairs, eight, then twelve, fanning into a semicircle through the undergrowth with slow coordination. My limiters held. My exoskeleton sat in its formal configuration, calm and inert. To them I must have smelled like an easy meal.

They had no idea what they were surrounding.

I turned my back on them and walked toward the castle.

Behind me, one hissed, a sharp rattling sound. Then there was silence, and the pale eyes winked out one pair at a time as the pack melted back into the Ashwood.

Animals always know.

Skeev scurried after me, flooded with relief. "Back to the castle, my lord?"

"Back to the castle. I'll rest. And in the morning I'll ask the Demon Lord whether I may go to the Spire."
