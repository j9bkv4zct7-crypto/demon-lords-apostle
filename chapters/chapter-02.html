Chapter 2

Blood in the Courtyard

2,980 words · about 13 min

‹ Chapter 1 Contents Chapter 3 ›

I didn't sleep that first night.

I sat cross-legged on the bed with my back to the cold wall, closed my eyes, and went looking for the thing the Demon Lord had put inside me.

At first there was nothing but darkness and the sound of my own breathing. Then I found it: a cold, impossibly dense knot behind my sternum, pulsing slowly like a second heart made of something that shouldn't exist.

I pulled on it.

Pain shot down both arms at once. When I opened my eyes, my veins had darkened under the skin into black rivers running from wrist to elbow. I kept pulling. Between my palms a sphere of dark matter formed, no bigger than a marble. The torchlight bent around it. That was gravitational lensing, the effect Professor Hanazawa had shown us on a slide only that afternoon, and now it was happening in my hands, in a stone room on another world.

I held it. Then I shaped it. I flattened it into a disc, drew it out into a needle, and folded it back into a sphere.

It fought me the whole time.

Dark matter had weight, and not only the physical kind. Holding it felt like keeping a wild animal on a leash made of thought. Each time my focus slipped, even a little, it destabilized and began to crackle with volatile energy. Twice it nearly detonated in my hands. Both times I caught it and forced it back into shape by will alone, sweating, teeth clenched.

Hours passed. My shirt soaked through. My hands shook. My mana drained and refilled, drained and refilled, and I learned the rhythm of it: how much I could pull before my body protested, and how long I could hold a construct before the strain turned dangerous. I took notes in my head the way I would have in a lab. Input, output, failure point. Repeat.

By midnight, the dagger that had taken me several seconds to form in the corridor came in under two.

In the last hours before dawn, I held two projectiles at once, one dense spinning orb above each palm, humming with quiet, destructive potential.

My body ached and my mana was dangerously low. But something had changed. The connection to that cold core no longer felt like wrestling. It felt like gripping a handle I knew.

Faint red light crept through the window. Dawn here was the color of a bruise.

Someone pounded on the iron door hard enough to rattle it.

"Get up, human." Shiryeon. "His Majesty wants you in the courtyard. Now."

I opened the door with a small orb of dark matter in one hand and tossed it lazily into the air. It fell back into my palm without a wobble.

"Ready for blood," I said, "just as you asked."

She stood with her arms crossed. Her amber eyes followed the orb up and down. She saw it: the smoothness of the construct, the lack of strain in my face. Her expression didn't change, but her gaze stayed on it half a second longer than it had the day before.

"You trained all night," she said. It wasn't a question.

She turned and walked. I followed.

The castle was busy that morning. Red-skinned ogres hauled axes as tall as I was. Goblin runners darted between their legs with scrolls and sacks. At every intersection stood a skeletal knight in black plate, empty eye sockets turning to follow me. Everyone looked at me. Some looked curious, and some looked like they were wondering how I'd taste.

We came out into a vast courtyard of cracked black stone under that endless red sky. In its center a training pit had been cut into the ground, a ring about fifty feet across.

The Demon Lord stood at the far edge with his hands clasped behind his back.

Beside him stood something else.

It was seven feet of matted brown fur, with the body of a man and the head of a bull. It carried a stone war hammer as big as my torso, and its breath steamed in the cold air. Its small black eyes found me at once.

"Your first real fight," Shiryeon said, stepping aside. "Lesser minotaur. Level five."

The Demon Lord's voice carried easily across the courtyard.

"Show me what my investment has purchased, Apostle." A pause. "Kill it."

The minotaur bellowed, stamped one hoof against the stone, and charged.

It was fast, faster than something that size had any right to be. The ground jumped under its hooves. I had perhaps two seconds.

Level one meant fifty points of mana, and I had spent the night learning exactly how far fifty points would stretch. Nowhere near far enough for a wall or a big blade. But mass is not the same thing as density, and it doesn't take a big bullet to kill a man. It takes a fast one in the right place.

I raised my hand and gathered the smallest amount of mana I could still shape: two needles, thinner than a sewing pin, compressed until they were barely visible. Then I gave each one an instruction, a flaw folded into its structure on purpose. When the needle came to a stop, it would collapse inward.

I snapped my fingers.

The needles crossed the distance like black lightning. The first punched through the minotaur's left eye and the second through its right. Its charge stuttered. Its hooves scraped the stone as it stumbled, bellowing, swinging the hammer blindly.

I closed my fist.

The needles imploded.

At level one, the effect was small. But it went off inside a skull. Two miniature collapses folded inward behind its eyes, and I heard a wet, terrible crunch as the minotaur's head caved in on itself like crushed fruit. Then the pressure let go all at once, and bone and gore sprayed across the pit.

The body stood upright for one grotesque second, headless, the hammer still clutched in its fist.

Then it fell forward and hit the stone with a thud that shook the ground.

Silence.

The goblins had stopped moving. The ogres stared. Even the skeletal knights seemed to tilt their helmets.

Shiryeon's lips had parted slightly. She caught herself and closed them, and her jaw tightened.

The Demon Lord hadn't moved. His gold eyes went to the corpse, then to me.

"Creative," he said simply. "And efficient."

A soft chime sounded at the back of my mind, and words scrolled past my vision:

Lesser Minotaur slain. Experience doubled. Level 1 → 2.

The cold core pulsed. It felt denser and hungrier than before, and I felt it grow.

Then I got greedy, which is the scientific method by another name.

The corpse lay there, several hundred kilos of biological mass, and the thing inside me was hungry. It seemed like a reasonable hypothesis. I stretched out a hand, and dark matter seeped from my palm like black smoke. It crawled over the stone and wrapped the minotaur's body in a thin, shimmering blanket of violet and black.

I focused and pulled inward. Break it down. Bring it to me.

Nothing happened.

The dark matter coiled and writhed around the body and simply sat there, destructive energy with no instruction it could understand. I pushed harder. My mana drained, the blanket crackled and destabilized, and then it collapsed into wisps of black vapor.

A sharp pain bloomed behind my eyes. That was the limit. I could shape it, throw it, and detonate it. Telling it to take matter apart and feed it to me was a different order of problem.

"Ambitious," the Demon Lord said. He had come down into the courtyard, and he didn't sound disappointed. He sounded interested. "But dark matter is not a parasite, Apostle. It is a force. At your level you command its shape and its impact. What you just attempted requires an understanding of density at the molecular level. Level thirty at minimum. Perhaps higher."

He looked down at the ruined corpse for a moment.

"Patience. You killed a level-five beast at level one, on your first morning. That is more than sufficient." He glanced at Shiryeon. "Take him to the Monster Glade. Let him hunt. I want him at level ten by the end of the week."

Shiryeon nodded. Her face gave nothing away, but her amber eyes kept drifting back to me.

"Move, human. We're burning daylight."

I grinned at her. "Lead the way, future Queen."

Her eye twitched. Her hand went to her dagger by pure reflex, and her knuckles whitened on the grip. For a moment she really did look ready to draw.

"Call me that again," she said through her teeth, "and I'll leave you in the Glade for the wolves."

Behind me I heard something rare: a single quiet breath through the Demon Lord's nose. It was almost a laugh.

"Try not to antagonize my commander before she's finished escorting you, Apostle," he said, already turning back toward the castle. "She is the only reason you won't die out there."

Shiryeon stalked off without waiting for me. I followed, still grinning.

We left by the rear gates, and the demon lands opened up in front of me: plains of scorched black earth, rock formations jutting up like broken teeth, and far off a line of dark, twisted trees against the red sky.

Shiryeon walked fast and said nothing. Two hours of silence went by before the land began to change. The black earth softened into dark soil, the twisted trees grew thicker and taller, and the air filled with chittering, growling, and branches snapping under things I couldn't see.

The Monster Glade.

She stopped at the tree line and turned to face me. The contempt was gone from her face. Now she looked like a professional.

"Listen carefully. The forest is divided into zones. The outer ring holds creatures between level one and ten. Go deeper and you hit fifteen to twenty-five. The core..." She paused. "Don't go to the core."

She drew both daggers.

"I'll observe. You fight. Ready?"

Something snarled in the undergrowth close by.

I closed my eyes.

I'd noticed it during the night without understanding it. Whenever something moved near me, the dark matter in my core shifted, like iron filings near a magnet. It wasn't true detection, more like a pond feeling a stone drop into it. I let my awareness spread outward and listened for ripples.

There were three nearby. No, four.

I opened my eyes. To the left, a goblin scout three feet tall crouched behind a rotting log with a crude spear. Overhead, a shadow wolf clung to a thick branch, black fur bristling and yellow eyes fixed on me. Behind a cluster of rocks to the right, two more goblins waited with rusty short swords.

I snapped my fingers three times, fast.

Two needles went through the scout's throat, and it dropped behind the log without a sound. One caught the shadow wolf in the skull as it leapt, and it went limp in midair and crashed into the undergrowth, dead before it landed. The last two goblins took a needle through each temple and crumpled together, their swords clattering on the stone.

That was five kills in five seconds, and it had barely dented my mana.

Level 2 → 3.

Shiryeon leaned against a tree with her arms crossed and her daggers still out. Her eyes were wide. She said nothing, but her grip on the daggers had loosened considerably.

Deeper in the forest, something larger was crashing through the trees. The footsteps were heavy, and there was more than one set.

It was a forest troll: nine feet of sickly grey-green skin crusted with moss and bark, arms so long its knuckles dragged, a mouth full of broken yellow teeth, and little eyes sunk deep in a misshapen skull. Two goblins rode on its shoulders like parasites, screeching and jabbing their spears at me.

I'd already started building something heavier while it was still out of sight, a thicker needle, denser, vibrating with compressed dark matter. It cost more than the small ones, but my level-three core could carry it. I fired the moment the troll cleared the trees.

The needle went in like a bullet and buried itself in its left eye socket. Its head snapped back and it staggered.

It didn't fall.

It roared, a wet and horrible sound, and tore the ruined eye out of its own face. Black blood poured down its cheek, and the tissue around the wound was already knitting closed. Trolls regenerate.

The goblins leapt from its shoulders and came at me from both sides with spears lowered. The troll found its feet, fixed its remaining eye on me with animal fury, swung one massive arm, tore a young tree out of the ground roots and all, and threw it at me.

So I had a tree, two spears, and a troll that healed, all arriving at once.

At the edge of my vision, Shiryeon pushed off her tree and tensed. She didn't step in.

"Show me what you've got, human," she muttered.

I felt my mouth stretch into something savage.

The tree came first. I threw myself left and felt it brush past my shoulder before it smashed into the ground behind me in a burst of splinters. My hands were already moving. I sent two needles out sideways in opposite directions and caught each goblin mid-stride, one through the throat and one through the chest. They dropped, and their spears rolled away across the forest floor.

Then I turned all my attention on the troll.

Six needles. Two went through its kneecaps, and it buckled and crashed forward onto its hands. One went into its remaining eye, and it screamed and thrashed blind, its huge arms ripping up earth and roots. Then I sent three more, one after another, at the same point in the dead center of its skull. Each one drilled deeper than the last. Its regeneration fought back, tissue closing desperately around every wound, but I was faster. The third needle broke through.

I closed my fist.

The troll's skull imploded with a sickening crunch. The great body convulsed twice and went still, face down in the dirt, and black blood spread out from it in a widening circle.

I was breathing hard. That barrage had cost me.

Level 3 → 5.

Shiryeon slid her daggers back into their sheaths, slowly.

"Not bad," she said quietly. "Not bad at all."

I bowed to her with a little flourish, then turned and went looking for the next one.

I'd like to say I hunted for the kingdom's sake, or for my liege's, or out of some cold strategic calculation. The truth is that by noon it had become addictive. Every kill came with that chime and a rush of cold strength, and every level made the next kill easier. I'd spent twenty-one years in a world where effort turned into results slowly, if it turned into anything at all. Here the feedback loop was immediate.

A pack of four shadow wolves came at me out of the undergrowth, and I put needles through their skulls before they could close the distance. A green-skinned orc warrior, level six, charged me with a battle cry and a jagged iron cleaver. I sidestepped the swing and drove a dark-matter blade up through its ribs. A nest of giant spiders, each the size of a large dog, dropped from the canopy on silk lines, and needles shredded their abdomens before their legs touched the ground.

Level 5 → 7.

My constructs came faster now, and denser. The needles hit harder. I could feel my ceiling rising with each level, as if the room I lived in kept growing taller.

Then the light began to fail. Whatever this world used for a sun was sinking. My body ached, my mana was low, and my stamina was gone after hours of nonstop fighting.

Shiryeon appeared at my side. "Enough. You've burned through most of your reserves. Push further in this state and you'll die."

Somewhere deep in the forest, something howled. It was far away, and it was enormous.

"That's the inner ring," she said, narrowing her eyes. "We leave. Now."

I didn't argue. I fell in close behind her.

She moved through the trees as if she'd walked them a thousand times, and she probably had. When we came out onto the black earth again, the sky had darkened to burgundy, and night was coming in.

The walk back was quiet. My mind replayed every kill, every needle, every detonation. Seven levels in one day.

"You fight like someone who has nothing to lose," Shiryeon said suddenly, without looking back. There was no judgment in it. It was an observation.

I didn't answer. She was more right than she knew. Yesterday I'd had a rent payment and a tired future, and nothing else. Nothing to lose was exactly what I'd had.

The castle rose ahead of us, its black spires clawing at the sky and blue light flickering in its windows. At the rear gate a goblin runner scurried up and handed Shiryeon a small scroll. She read it quickly.

"His Majesty wants a report on your progress." She tucked the scroll away and glanced back at me. "I'll tell him level seven in one day."

She stopped where the corridor forked, one way toward my quarters and the other toward the throne room.

"Rest. Eat. Recover your mana. Tomorrow we go deeper."

She turned to leave. Then she stopped.

"You did well today, human."

Then she was gone, her boots echoing away down the stone.

I stood alone in the blue torchlight for a while, listening to my second heart beat cold and steady under my ribs.
