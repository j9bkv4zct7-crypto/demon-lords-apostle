Chapter 11

Prince of the Abyss

5,020 words · about 22 min

‹ Chapter 10 Contents Chapter 12 ›

I opened the door and called down the empty corridor.

Small bare feet pattered on stone, fast. Skeev came around the corner at full speed, nearly hit the wall, caught himself, and straightened up as tall as his hunched frame allowed.

"Lord Mephesto! Skeev is here! Skeev was nearby, polishing the torches in the east corridor. Not sleeping. Definitely not sleeping." His nostrils flared, and his ears twitched. He tilted his head. "Lord Mephesto smells different. Like... bones singing? Skeev does not know how to say it. But something is different. Deep different." He shook it off and snapped to attention. "What does Lord Mephesto need? Food? A message? Skeev is the fastest messenger in the whole castle. Commander Shiryeon said so once." A pause. "Well. She said Skeev was the least slow. But Skeev counts that."

Before I answered, I tried something.

For days the exoskeleton had been either armor or nothing. I closed my eyes, pictured what a demon prince ought to wear, and pushed the image into the clusters as a feeling as much as a design.

Dark matter flowed from my pores like liquid shadow and solidified as it met the air. A long black coat came first, high-collared and falling to mid-calf, its surface shifting between matte and a faint oil-on-water iridescence. At the back it split into two tails that drifted slightly with no wind to move them. Fine patterns etched themselves along the hems and cuffs, fractal and branching, the geometry of my own channels written on the cloth. Beneath it, a fitted tunic formed with a faint raised texture like dragon scale. Sleek, angular pauldrons crystallized on my shoulders with purple-black veins of mana pulsing slowly through them like living stone. Fitted trousers followed, and boots armored at the shin in the same crystal.

I didn't wear it. I grew it.

I filed the configuration away and named it: Casual Mode.

Skeev's eyes had gone as wide as saucers. His mouth hung open.

"Lord... Lord Mephesto..." he stammered. "Skeev has seen the Demon Lord in his war raiment. Skeev has seen Commander Shiryeon in her battle plate." He swallowed. "Lord Mephesto looks like a prince of the abyss. Skeev is not worthy to stand in the same corridor."

He dropped to one knee on pure instinct, then scrambled back up with his ears flat in embarrassment.

"Food," I said. "Please."

"Food! Skeev fetches food! Best food!" He took off down the corridor, slapping stone, and nearly crashed into the wall at the corner. His voice came echoing back from somewhere deep in the castle.

"BRIS! BRIS, WAKE UP! LORD MEPHESTO NEEDS FOOD AND HE LOOKS LIKE A GOD!"

By the time I reached the dining hall, a long chamber with one massive stone table under iron chandeliers of blue-black flame, Skeev was already standing by the kitchen entrance with his chest heaving. An ogre guard in the corridor had done a double take and pressed itself flat against the wall to let me pass.

Bris came through the kitchen door a moment later under a platter nearly as wide as she was tall: roasted shadowboar haunch in a dark, sweet glaze, charred roots, a stack of steaming flatbread, a bowl of thick purple broth that smelled of iron and herbs, and a small dish of crystallized mana-fruit, each piece no bigger than a grape and glowing faintly blue. She set it down with a thud. Her arms were trembling slightly, and it wasn't from the weight.

She was staring at me, her eyes traveling from the boots to the pauldrons to the high collar.

"I made extra," she said quietly. "I thought Master might be very hungry after... whatever made him look like that."

Skeev stood beside her with his arms crossed, nodding as if he'd personally orchestrated the meal. "Skeev told Bris to use the good meat. The reserve cuts. Skeev authorized it."

"Skeev did not authorize anything," Bris said flatly. "Skeev screamed in my ear while I was sleeping."

"Skeev was urgent. Urgency requires volume."

I sat down, and the coat split naturally around the chair. The shadowboar was nearly black at the center, rich and dense, the glaze cutting through the heaviness, and my blood went to work on it at once. A mana-fruit dissolved on my tongue like cold sugar, and I felt the ripple of it all the way down to the anchors.

"Skeev," I said between bites. "Tell me about the kingdom. From your point of view."

His ears twitched in opposite directions. "From... Skeev's point of view?" He scratched behind one ear. "No one has asked Skeev that before. Not ever."

He hopped up onto the bench across from me with his feet dangling, folded his hands on the stone, and frowned in intense concentration.

"Skeev was born in the lower warrens. Under the castle. Tunnels and caves, carved long ago. Hundreds of goblins down there. Maybe thousands. Skeev does not count well." He paused. "Is not a bad life. Not good either. Goblins are lowest. Everyone knows this. Orcs are soldiers. Demons are commanders. Ogres are siege weapons. Kobolds dig and mine. Imps cook and build." He glanced at Bris, who lingered in the kitchen doorway. "Goblins do whatever is left."

He picked at a scratch on the table.

"The Demon Lord is fair. Skeev will say that honestly. He does not allow killing your own kind without reason. Before him, before Skeev was born, the old stories say orcs ate goblins when food was scarce. The Demon Lord stopped that. Made laws. All monsters are citizens of the kingdom. All have purpose." His yellow eyes came up. "But purpose is not the same as respect. Goblins have purpose. Goblins do not have respect."

In the doorway, Bris crossed her arms tightly over her apron.

"Same for imps," she said quietly. "I cook for the castle. Every meal, every day. The officers eat what I make and never look at me. I'm furniture that produces food."

Skeev nodded hard. "Is like that. But the kingdom is better than the wild. In the wild, goblins are hunted by everything. Humans especially." Something hard came into his eyes that I'd never seen there before. "Humans come into the Monster Glade for sport. Kill goblins for experience points. Skeev lost clutchmates to human adventurers. They do not even take the bodies. Just kill and move on. Like goblins are practice targets."

He was quiet for a moment.

"That is why the war started. The Demon Lord said enough. Said monsters are not prey." He looked at me, fierce and raw. "Skeev believes that. The Demon Lord gave goblins a place. A home. Laws that say Skeev's life has value. Is not perfect. Skeev still gets kicked by orcs sometimes. Still gets called runt. But Skeev is alive. Skeev's clutchmates in the wild are not."

He sat up straight on the bench.

"That is the kingdom from Skeev's point of view. Is hard. Is unfair sometimes. But is ours."

The blue-black flames flickered in the chandeliers. Bris stood in the doorway with her arms wrapped around herself and her eyes glistening.

"I agree," she said simply, and went back into the kitchen.

Skeev watched her go, and his ears drooped. "Lord Mephesto is the first officer to ask Skeev these things," he said quietly. "First to eat with Skeev. First to say thank you to Bris." His hands pressed flat against the stone. "Skeev does not forget this. Ever."

I thought of the goblin scout I'd put two needles through in the Glade on my second day in this world, without a second thought. Wild, not one of the kingdom's. I didn't say it out loud.

"Thank you for telling me, Skeev," I said. Then, louder, toward the kitchen: "And thank you for the food, Bris."

Skeev's ears shot straight up. His eyes went wide and wet. He opened his mouth, closed it, and just nodded, rapidly, clutching the edge of the table as if it was the only thing holding him to the floor.

From the kitchen came a small voice, barely audible. "I heard. I... thank you too, Master."

It was followed immediately by the sound of a pot being scrubbed with tremendous violence.

Sereth was already at her workbench in the lower west tower when I came in. She stopped writing mid-stroke. Her black eyes traveled over the coat, the fractal hems, and the pulsing pauldrons.

"You restructured the exoskeleton into an aesthetic output layer," she said flatly. "Intent-based morphological control. On your own." She came around the bench and circled me slowly, studying the way the coat-tails moved. "The anchors are singing. I can hear them from here. All fourteen. Fully propagated." Two cold fingers pressed against my wrist, and her probe slid into my channels like a needle of ice. My clusters rippled outward from the contact in rings. She breathed in sharply. "Forty-eight. Confirmed. The lattice is cleaner than anything I have built by hand." Something rare flickered behind her eyes. "You are ready."

She opened a journal thick with spirals and branching fractals.

"The first layer. Right now your clusters process mana linearly: intake, convert, distribute, expend. Efficient but flat. We are encoding a feedback architecture. Every cluster returns a fraction of its output into its own intake before distributing the rest." She drew a circle in the air with one pale finger. "Each cycle produces slightly more than the last. It is not explosive. It is a controlled, perpetual climb. Output, reinforcement, regeneration, all of it rising with every heartbeat." She lifted a syringe of shimmering black liquid threaded with silver filaments that seemed to move on their own inside the glass. "Catalytic primer. It activates the loop at all fourteen anchors at once. It will hurt. Significantly."

"Ready."

The coat went still. The hems flattened and hardened, and the pauldrons locked. I lay back on the obsidian slab.

"The primers must fire within six seconds of one another, or the loop cascades unevenly and collapses. Which means..." She raised one hand, and ghostly white threads of mana unspooled from each of her fingers and fastened onto fourteen syringes. They lifted from the tray and arranged themselves in the air above me, each one poised over an anchor. "...I inject all fourteen at once. When the primer reaches the anchors, every cluster in your body will try to rewrite its processing cycle at the same moment. Your nervous system will read it as systemic trauma. Do not move. Do not resist. If you fight it, the loop fragments."

The threads went taut.

"Three. Two. One."

Fourteen needles plunged through dark matter, skin, and muscle and into drake bone.

For half a second, nothing happened.

Then the world turned white.

It was everywhere at once. My bones vibrated at a pitch that made my teeth feel ready to shatter. My channels spasmed like a seizing heart. Every cluster in my body was rewriting its fundamental pattern while I was awake and alive and felt every rewrite as a hot wire threaded through my marrow. My back arched off the slab and my jaw locked. My fingers clawed at the stone, and the dark matter over my knuckles cracked, spiderwebbing, before the clusters frantically sealed it.

The anchors were screaming, and I could hear it, a high harmonic whine in my skull, my teeth, the fluid of my eyes.

"Hold," Sereth said from very far away. "Rewrite at nineteen percent."

My vision split into layers: the ceiling, the blue-green fire, her pale face above me with the threads still running from her fingertips into my body.

"Thirty-one."

Something gave deep in my chest. The clusters around the sternum finished first and immediately fed their output back into their own intake. The loop caught. I felt a pulse of mana that had come from nowhere outside me, self-generated and recursive. My sternum hummed.

"First loop confirmed. Thirteen remaining."

Don't resist. I forced my hands flat against the obsidian. My knuckles cracked and repaired, cracked and repaired, a cycle of fracture and rebuilding that mirrored what was happening in every cell.

The left scapula caught, and the agony in that shoulder turned to pressure, then to a deep, thrumming warmth. Then both femurs went at once. My heels slammed into the slab hard enough to crack it, and the drake bone in my thighs rang all the way up into my hip sockets.

"Forty-four percent. The cascade is accelerating. Hold."

The ribs fired in sequence, one, two, three, four, like a fuse burning up my side, each locking with a thud I felt in my skull. Then the right radius, and my right arm seized so violently that the gauntlet exploded off my hand in a spray of black crystal across the slab.

And the fragments came back. They reversed direction and slid across the obsidian toward my hand, crawling home, while the covering regrew twice as fast as before.

Sereth's breath caught. "Self-reclamation," she murmured. She wasn't talking to me anymore. She was talking to her journals, to the future. "Emergent behavior. Left radius... confirming..."

"Seventy-eight."

The right clavicle caught with a chord only I could hear, deep and resonant, and a visible wave of dark matter rolled beneath my skin from shoulder to fingertips and back. The right scapula followed.

"Eighty-nine. Two remaining."

The cervical anchor at the base of my skull went next, a bright, clean note, and then there was only one left: the left clavicle, the first anchor she'd ever set, at the junction where my shoulders met my chest and my neck met my torso and every channel in my upper body converged on a single point. It was the keystone.

The primer hit it.

My vision went black this time, not white. It was an absolute void. At the center of everything I felt the keystone pulling data from all thirteen active loops at once, cross-referencing them, harmonizing them, and...

It caught.

Fourteen loops snapped into a single rhythm. The whine in my skull collapsed into one bass note so deep the slab shook beneath me.

"One hundred percent," Sereth whispered. "All fourteen confirmed. The first layer is live."

The color came back sharper than ever before. The fire burned like molten copper, and Sereth's translucent skin was a galaxy of tiny detail. Every heartbeat compounded on the one before it, a tide rising a millimeter at a time, constant and relentless.

Sereth caught herself against her bench.

"Extraordinary."

I lay there with my chest heaving. "We discussed two layers. My body can take two at the current architecture."

"You remember correctly." She flipped back through her journal to a page with two spirals in heavy ink and a third sketched faintly with a large X through it. "Two is the ceiling, given your current density and anchor count. A third is impossible for now." She held a small crystal near my chest. It vibrated, slowed, and quickened again in an unsteady rhythm. "But the first must reach harmonic equilibrium before I build on it. Encoding a second layer on an oscillating first is building a tower on wet sand. Three hours. Perhaps less, knowing your biology."

She sat down heavily in her chair. For the first time since I'd known her, Sereth Vaun looked tired. Fourteen remote injections at once had cost her something.

"Move. Use your mana. Test the new output, but no combat and no maximum exertion. Let the loops find their rhythm." Her pen began to move. "The second-layer primer must be stronger, to overwhelm the first layer's resistance. It will hurt more. Significantly more. The first layer rewrote your clusters' base cycle. The second will rewrite the rewrite." A faint, grim smile. "Recursive pain. Poetic, given the procedure." She waved me toward the door. "Go. Eat again if you are hungry; your caloric demands are about to rise substantially. And, Mephesto."

I stopped at the door.

The black eyes held mine over the journal.

"What you endured just now, without sedation, would have killed most organisms. Remember that."

She looked down and went on writing.

"Skeev," I said in the corridor. I didn't raise my voice. He was never far.

Eleven seconds later, a vent grate near the floor popped open, and Skeev tumbled out in a heap of green limbs and oversized ears. He scrambled up, brushed off his loincloth, and snapped to attention.

"Skeev is here! Skeev heard Lord Mephesto from two corridors away. Skeev has developed a very good ear for Lord Mephesto's voice specifically." His nostrils flared. "Lord Mephesto feels... heavier. Not in body. In the air around Lord Mephesto. Like standing near a furnace."

The first layer was already putting out enough ambient pressure for a goblin to feel it.

"Show me the kingdom," I said. "Beyond the castle. I want to see everything."

His ears shot straight up. "Everything? A full tour?" He clasped his hands. "Skeev knows every tunnel, every street, every alley, every rooftop. Skeev will show Lord Mephesto things even officers do not know about."

He set off at a near run, remembered himself, and dropped back into proper escort position, half a step behind me on my left. He did it with visible effort, his excitement fighting protocol.

We went down through the castle, past armories where kobold smiths hammered dark iron and barracks where orcs sat sharpening blades. They looked up as we passed, and I caught fragments.

That's the Apostle.

Smells different today. Stronger.

Harren. One night. Alone.

At the main gate, thirty feet of black iron, one of the ogre sentinels actually inclined its massive head.

Outside, the kingdom fell away beneath the castle in tiers. The first was military: barracks, armories, and siege workshops cut straight into volcanic rock, orc platoons drilling in unison, and catapults and ballistae lining the outer wall, aimed at the horizon. The second, down a wide stone ramp, was everything else.

"This is the Sprawl," Skeev said, sweeping an arm wide. "Where everyone lives who is not a soldier or an officer. Goblins, imps, kobolds, lesser demons, beastkin, some ogres retired from service. Is messy. Is loud. Is home."

It was loud. A dozen monster tongues overlapped, barking and chittering and laughing. A kobold haggled with a horned beastkin over iron ore, and three goblin children chased one another across a rooftop.

"Take me somewhere I can buy artifacts," I said. "A trade house."

"Trade house?" He grinned, showing small sharp teeth. "Monsters do not use fancy human words. Here it is called the Hollow."

He cut down an alley so tight my pauldrons would have scraped the walls if the dark matter hadn't flattened itself to fit. Then it opened onto a sunken plaza carved into the rock, circular, with steps descending like an amphitheater. Its stepped walls were lined with stalls and shops built from scavenged timber and dark iron and draped with hide. The air was thick with ozone, hot metal, and the sharp chemical tang of enchantment residue. Mana hummed through my clusters, coming from everything at once.

"Everything here is trade," Skeev said. "No gold, like humans use. You trade what you have for what you want: materials, services, favors. Mana crystals are closest to money. Everyone takes them." He pointed as we went down. "Weapons there. Battlefield salvage, refitted, there. That is Ishvane's second stall; her apprentice runs it. And there..."

He pointed at the far end, where a real shop had been cut deep into the rock face. A heavy iron door stood half open beneath a single word etched in jagged demon script. Relics.

His voice dropped. "Mordekai's den. A collector. Rare things, from ruins and dungeons all across Jahara. Very expensive. Very dangerous. Mordekai is a greater demon. An old one. He was a commander until he lost an arm at the Siege of Kethara, and the Demon Lord let him retire, out of respect." Lower still. "Mordekai does not like small talk. Does not like haggling. Does not like most people. But Mordekai respects power, and anyone the Demon Lord respects." He looked up at me meaningfully. "Lord Mephesto should be fine."

The monsters parted around me as I crossed the Hollow. It wasn't fear, exactly. It was awareness.

The shop was dim and cool. Its walls were shelves cut floor to ceiling into the rock and crowded with rings, amulets, gauntlets, and crystals in iron cages. On the far wall hung a sword humming with such dense enchantment that my clusters physically turned toward it.

Behind a stone counter sat Mordekai. He was seven feet tall even seated, with thick, scarred crimson skin. His left arm ended at the elbow in an old, clean stump, and the right was massive and corded and resting on the counter. He had a broad, hard face, deep-set black eyes under a heavy brow, and two horn stumps jutting from his forehead, filed or shattered, impossible to say which.

"Apostle." His voice was stones grinding together. He didn't stand. "Heard about Harren. Heard about you. What do you want."

"Browsing," I said. "I haven't really left the castle. I want to see what the kingdom has to offer."

Skeev, I noticed, had not come in. His yellow eyes peered over the threshold.

"Browsing," Mordekai repeated, as if chewing gravel. "This is not a curiosity shop, Apostle. Everything in here has killed someone. Most of it has killed many someones."

I walked the shelves slowly. My clusters reacted to each artifact differently. Some pulled at my mana, some pushed it away, and some threw up interference that made the new loop in my bones stutter for a microsecond. A ring carved from a single piece of black diamond gave off a cold I felt from two feet away, and frost crawled across the shelf around it in fine fractals.

"Rimeclaw Band," Mordekai said behind me without turning. "From a frost wyrm's hoard in the northern wastes. Freezes anything the wearer touches. Its last owner lost three fingers learning that."

A gauntlet on a pedestal was matte black, veined with red, and pulsing slowly. "Siegebreaker. Multiplies striking force eightfold. One punch per day; then it needs a day to recharge. It breached the outer wall at Kethara." His stump twitched. "I was on the other side of that wall."

Then a thin silver chain holding a small, perfectly round black pearl. My clusters went haywire near it. The loop spiked, oscillated wildly, and settled into a strange new harmony I had never felt. The pearl seemed to drink the light from the air around it.

"Do not touch that." It was the first real emotion I'd heard in his voice. "Voidstone Pendant. It absorbs incoming magic and converts it into raw mana for the wearer. Extremely powerful. Extremely unstable. It does not distinguish between hostile magic and ambient mana. Wear it too long, and it starts feeding on your life."

I drew my hand back, and the clusters calmed.

Last was the sword on the wall. It was a longsword with a dark blade, a wrapped leather grip, and no crossguard. It was plain, almost humble, and yet the air around it shimmered like heat haze. It was humming, and the tone matched the frequency of my loop almost exactly.

"You feel that one." Mordekai's chair creaked. "The only piece in this shop I have never been able to price. From a sealed vault beneath the ruins of Vel'Kathar, a kingdom that fell before the Empire existed. No name. No inscription. No maker's mark. Three enchanters have examined it, and none could find the bottom of its layers." He stood at last, all seven feet of scarred crimson, and came to stand beside me. "It answers mana-dense bodies. Every demon who has held it felt nothing. A faint hum, at best." His black eyes slid to me. "You are four feet away, and it is already singing for you. I can hear it." A pause. "Interested? Or still browsing?"

"A question," I said. "What happens when the Voidstone gets its fill?"

His eyes narrowed. "Most people ask the price. Or how to use it without dying. You ask what happens when it is full." He leaned back against the counter. "Truthfully, I do not know. Neither did the three owners before me. It has never reached capacity. Every wielder who tried either died of exposure or took it off first. It just keeps drinking." His jaw worked. "There is an old theory, from the Vel'Kathar texts. The same ruins as that sword. A Voidstone Bloom. At true saturation, the pearl inverts. It stops absorbing and begins emitting, and releases everything it has ever consumed in a single burst, in every direction." He let that hang. "After centuries of absorption across multiple owners? It could level a city. Crack a mountain. Or do nothing; no one has seen it. It may also simply have no limit, in which case filling it is not the concern. Surviving it is." He looked at me. "Why. You are thinking of feeding it."

"I am. If I'm stronger than the stone, I should be able to incorporate it into my power and give it set commands, instead of letting it act on its own."

For the first time, something moved in his face. It wasn't surprise. It looked more like recalculation.

"Dominate an artifact. Not wear it. Bend it." He folded his one arm across his chest. "That is not how artifacts work. They do not obey. The Voidstone absorbs; you cannot tell it to stop any more than you can tell fire not to burn." His eyes went to my pauldrons, the mana-light in the crystal, and the pressure coming off me. "Unless. Unless you do not mean to command the enchantment. You mean to wrap it. Encase it in something stronger than its own field, a housing that opens only when you command it. Closed, it is inert. Open, it feeds on what you choose to give it." He tapped a broken horn. "Something like that exoskeleton. In theory, and only in theory, because no one has ever tried it. But if your housing is a fraction weaker than its pull, it eats through the shell and starts on your exoskeleton. Then your clusters. Then your blood. Then you. And it would be stronger for it." In the doorway, Skeev made a small strangled noise, and Mordekai ignored him. "So the theory is sound. The question is whether you are strong enough, right now, to overpower a thing that has been drinking for centuries."

He let the silence stretch. The pearl seemed to pulse, once, faintly, as if it knew it was being discussed.

"Are you?"

I could feel the answer already. The first layer pushed mana outward in compounding waves, and the Voidstone pulled inward, constant and steady. Push and pull. My clusters ran the differential in real time. At one layer, my output beat its hunger, but only just. One fluctuation or one lapse in attention, and it would tip.

After the second layer, the gap would widen with every heartbeat.

"Not yet," I said.

His brow rose a fraction.

"But soon."

The hard mask cracked, very slightly. One corner of his mouth twitched. It wasn't a smile. It was acknowledgment.

"First honest answer I have heard in this shop in six years. Most would have grabbed it and died. You measured yourself against it and found the gap." He returned to his chair. "The Voidstone stays on the shelf. When you are ready, if you are ever ready, come back. I will sell it to no one else." He sat heavily. "Now. The sword?"

"I'll take the Voidstone."

He blinked, once, slowly.

"Did you not just tell me you are not strong enough yet?"

"I'll store it for now. It's an experiment for later."

"M-my lord," Skeev stammered from the doorway, "perhaps the sword would be..."

The pearl pulsed again, and I felt its pull lapping at the edges of my exoskeleton like dark water at a shore. The first layer held it off. Barely.

"Let me be clear," Mordekai said, low and heavy. "I am not refusing the sale. You are the Demon Lord's Apostle. If you want it, you take it. That is how power works. But I have held that thing in my hand and felt it try to drink the marrow out of my bones. Do not put it on until you are certain. Carry it. Store it. Seal it in dark matter, if you can. Do not wear it."

"Understood."

He nodded once. He'd said his piece. He lifted the pendant from its bracket by the chain, never touching the pearl itself, and the light in the shop dimmed noticeably as it left the wall. He laid it on a square of folded black cloth.

"I do not accept gold for this. Never have. It is not a commodity. It is a liability with potential. I kept it because no one worthy ever walked in." He pushed the cloth toward me. "Take it. Consider the debt open. One day I will ask you for something, and you will remember that Mordekai gave you a weapon that could crack mountains. That is how I do business."

I lifted it by the chain. The dark matter on my fingers tightened and densified on instinct, putting a barrier between its field and my skin. The pull was there, constant and patient, like holding a sleeping predator by the scruff. I folded it into the cloth and formed a dense pocket of dark matter at my hip, compressed and sealed, cutting off every thread of ambient mana. The field went silent.

From the doorway, Skeev let out a breath he seemed to have been holding since we came in.

"Good," Mordekai said, settling back. "Now get out of my shop, before you decide you want the sword too and I have to explain why that one has a price."

It might have been humor. It might not.
