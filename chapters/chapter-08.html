Chapter 8

Thirteen Minutes

2,953 words · about 13 min

‹ Chapter 7 Contents Interlude: The Report ›

I stood and stretched. With one hundred percent integration, every fiber answered at once, with no lag and no resistance. It felt like precision engineering wrapped in living tissue.

"Stay here," I said. "I'm about to go all out."

Shiryeon looked at me. "Alone?"

"Stay here."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she stepped back, leaned against her tree, crossed her arms, and bent one knee. It was the posture of someone settling in to watch a performance.

"Don't die," she said flatly.

There was one more thing I wanted first. I pushed a standing order deep into the cluster architecture, below conscious thought: any threat that entered kill range would trigger a tendril that struck to kill and withdrew, with no thought and no hesitation, the way a reflex works. I tested it by flicking a pebble past the edge of my vision. A tendril burst from my back, turned the stone to dust in midair, and was gone in under a quarter of a second. I hadn't even blinked.

Autonomous defense was online.

Below me, the last patrol turned toward the barracks, and the ten-minute gap began.

I triggered Battle Mode. The pre-formed plates rose from under my skin and locked into place in a rattle of clicks: chest, shoulders, greaves, the horned helm sealing over my skull, the arm blades sliding out and humming. The tendrils spread wide behind me like dark wings against the night.

I stepped off the ridge.

Five hundred yards of freefall. Wind screamed past the helm. The depot swelled beneath me: tents, warehouses, wagons, and soldiers strolling between pools of torchlight without once looking up.

I hit the center of the supply yard like a meteor.

The crater was fifteen feet across. The shockwave flattened the nearest tents and sent three wagons tumbling end over end. Soldiers were thrown off their feet as dust and debris boiled into the sky.

For two seconds there was silence. Then the screaming started.

A soldier staggered up ten yards away with his sword half-drawn and his mouth opening to shout the alarm. A tendril went through his chest before the first syllable came out, and withdrew. He dropped.

Then they came from everywhere. Eight infantry charged from the east in a tight formation with swords raised. My blades opened the first two before they registered that I'd moved. A horizontal cut took the third from hip to shoulder, and four tendrils found four throats and hearts. Eight bodies hit the ground in under two seconds.

More were pouring out of the barracks.

I crouched and launched myself.

The ground spiderwebbed where my feet had been. I went in spinning, tendrils flattened along my body and spiraling with me, arm blades out, until I was a single rotating edge of dark matter. I was a drill.

I crossed sixty yards in under a second and went through the barracks lengthwise.

It was a long timber hall. I tore through the front wall as if it were paper. Men were still pulling on armor and reaching for weapon racks, and none of them finished. I punched out through the back wall and skidded to a stop with my boots carving twin furrows in the dirt. Behind me, the barracks folded in on itself. A shattered oil lamp caught, and flames began licking through the wreckage.

Infantry ×26 slain. Level 21 → 25.

Power surged through me, and the arm blades grew another six inches without my asking.

Three sharp horn blasts sounded. That was the alarm.

Forty figures came out of a stone building near the river, and they didn't move like infantry. They wore robes instead of armor and carried staves, and their hands were already glowing. They formed a crescent with practiced speed, front rank kneeling and rear rank lifting staves, and forty voices began a single chant.

A beam of white-hot fire, forty mages poured into one strike, came screaming across the yard and turned the ground to glass in its path.

The barrier took it.

It shoved me back through the dirt six inches, twelve, eighteen. Heat bled through. The clusters threw power at the contact point, and the beam split around me in two scorching streams that blackened the earth on either side.

It held.

Through the dying flames I watched their faces change. Forty mages, one unified attack, and I was still standing.

Behind me a tendril fired on its own at something I never saw, and I heard a body drop.

The mages began a second chant. I didn't let them finish.

I raised both arms, and needles burst from my palms like a shotgun blast: hundreds of them, each no thicker than a sewing pin, each carrying a compressed core of unstable mana. They spread in a wide cone and filled the air between us like black rain falling sideways. A few mages threw up personal shields. A few dove aside. Most were still mid-chant.

The needles punched through robes and skin, so small that a few of the mages looked down at the pinpricks in confusion.

Then the cores detonated.

The crescent vanished in a cascading chain of blasts, hundreds of small explosions overlapping and compounding. Shields burst from internal pressure they had never been designed to stop. The stone building behind them cracked down the middle. When the dust settled, there was a field of smoking craters where a disciplined formation had stood. One mage at the far edge, the only one who'd thrown herself clear, was dragging herself through the dirt toward a staff that wasn't there anymore.

A tendril fired. She stopped moving.

Combat Mages ×40 slain. Level 25 → 36.

Eleven levels at once hit me like lightning. The clusters screamed with new capacity, restructuring and expanding in real time. My mana pool nearly doubled. The barrier compressed until the air around me visibly shimmered, and my drake bones hummed.

The depot was burning, the barracks were gone, and the soldiers who were left had split into two groups: the few running toward me with desperate courage, and the many running away.

Then a voice cut through it all.

"HOLD THE LINE!"

He came out of the largest tent: tall and broad, in polished plate trimmed with gold, a blue cape streaming behind him, a greatsword with an edge that glowed faintly white. He had a weathered face, a jaw set like iron, short grey hair, and cold blue eyes that found me at once through the smoke.

The captain. Twelve soldiers in heavier armor formed up around him with locked shields. These were veterans.

"What in the goddess's name are you," he breathed, staring at the horns, the violet eyes, and the swaying tendrils. Then his grip tightened on the sword, and he planted his feet. "It doesn't matter. You will not take Harren."

The twelve hit me as a wall of steel with overlapping shields, moving in perfect step, while the captain flanked to my right with his blade blazing white.

My tendrils slammed down on the shield wall. Two men crumpled under them, and a third took a point through the gap between shield rim and helm. The other nine adapted, angling their shields up so the tendrils glanced off. The steel was enchanted and blessed.

Then the captain struck.

He was faster than any man in full plate had a right to be. The white edge bit into my barrier and actually went through the outer layer. Pain flickered across my left shoulder, and there was a shallow cut in the exoskeleton.

He'd drawn blood.

The clusters flagged him at once: level twenty-eight, with a divine blessing on the blade, anti-demon work that partially bypassed dark-matter defenses. Two autonomous tendrils speared for his neck. He rolled with impossible agility, and they buried themselves in the dirt.

"Blessed steel, abomination," he snarled, resetting his stance. "The goddess protects."

All right, then. Time to get serious.

The excitement drained out of me, and something colder took its place.

The arm blades withdrew into my forearms with a sharp hiss. I poured the dark matter from both channels into my right hand and compressed it, layer upon layer, into something thin rather than wide. It became a katana, built molecule by molecule, each layer packed to an absurd density. The mana inside it thrummed with barely contained force. The air around the edge warped, and light bent away from it as though afraid to touch it. One swing of it could have leveled a hillside.

At the same time I rewrote the armor. Bulk stripped away from the chest and shoulders and flowed into the joints: ankles, knees, hips, elbows, wrists. It went from tank to predator, lighter and leaner, the helm thinning to something sleek and the tendrils slimming to trade crushing power for speed.

It took two seconds. The captain was in mid-swing when I finished.

I stepped aside. The greatsword passed through the place my chest had been a heartbeat earlier and cut nothing but smoke. His eyes widened. I had been standing still, and then I simply wasn't there.

I was beside him.

The nine elites reacted professionally, their shields pivoting and their weapons thrusting toward my new position. They weren't fast enough. I went through them.

The katana sang. One horizontal arc passed through the first man's shield, his armor, and his body, through the man behind him, and into a third. The cut was so clean that the halves stood upright for a full second before they slid apart. A spear came for my throat, and I tilted my head two inches and let it go by my ear. A tendril snapped his wrist, and the return stroke took his head. Five, six, seven. I moved among them like smoke, and every one of them fell to a single motion. Their blessed shields always faced where I had been, never where I was. Eight, split from crown to groin. Nine, her sword arm pinned by a tendril while the katana opened her throat.

Three seconds. Nine veterans.

Elite Infantry ×9 slain. Level 36 → 42.

The world slowed around me as my processing outran it.

The captain stood alone. He had just watched nine of his best die in three seconds, and his greatsword trembled. It wasn't weakness. It was rage. The muscles stood out in his neck like cords.

"You butcher," he whispered.

He raised the sword overhead. The white glow intensified, brighter than ever. He was pouring everything he had into a single strike, and the ground cracked beneath his feet while his armor groaned.

"I'm not dumb enough to let you build strength," I said.

Mana flooded my legs, and the ground shattered into a crater where I'd stood. I crossed the distance between us faster than a blink. The world smeared, and the wind didn't have time to move.

He was still raising the sword, and the glow was still gathering. His eyes were still fixed on the place where I'd been.

I was already past him.

The katana finished its arc. It went through the blessed greatsword first, and the enchanted steel parted like wet cloth, its white glow guttering out as the upper half spun away trailing sparks. The blade didn't slow. It passed through his gorget and his neck, clean.

I stopped three paces behind him with the katana held low, still humming.

His body stood for two full seconds. The two halves of his sword clattered to the ground. His mouth opened, and no sound came out.

Then his head slid off his shoulders and hit the dirt, and his body crumpled sideways into the mud. The gold trim caught the firelight one last time.

Captain Aldous Marrow (Lv. 28) slain.

The system gave me his name only after he was dead.

Silence fell across the depot. The men who were left, scattered and hiding and fleeing, had all seen it: their captain, their strongest, ended by a single cut. I could hear the fight go out of them. Swords hit the ground. Boots ran. Someone was sobbing.

The katana dissolved back into my arm.

Harren's military capacity was gone. Its infrastructure wasn't, yet.

I launched myself straight up.

The speed configuration turned the leap into a rocket launch. Fifty feet, a hundred, two hundred. The burning depot shrank beneath me, and Harren spread out like a map. The military section was clearly divided from the civilian village by rows of tents, warehouses, and wagon lines along the river.

I could see everything: three grain warehouses on the north side, a weapons cache in a reinforced stone building beside the ruined barracks, medical tents clustered near the water, twelve transport wagons along the main road, a siege yard on the eastern edge full of catapult frames, battering-ram parts, and crates of iron bolts, and two wooden bridges carrying the supply road east toward the front.

I raised both arms. Dark matter poured from every pore as all my clusters worked in concert, and the needles formed by the hundreds, then by the thousands. These were thicker than the ones I'd used on the mages, each packed with enough force to blow a wagon apart.

I hung at the top of the arc for one perfect second like a dark star, violet light from my helm glowing against the smoke below.

Then I brought my arms down.

The barrage fell in precise clusters, each group assigned its own target by my sharpened eyes. The grain warehouses didn't collapse. They vaporized, three blasts that flung burning grain across a hundred yards and started a dozen secondary fires. The weapons cache held for a fraction of a second before its walls bulged outward and came apart, sending steel and stone skyward in a column of fire. The medical tents simply ceased to exist. The twelve wagons went up one after another down the main road, like footsteps walking away. The siege yard got the heaviest share. Catapult frames shattered, the ram split lengthwise, and the bolt crates turned into shrapnel that shredded everything within fifty yards.

The bridges came last. Two precise clusters struck the center spans at once, and planks and pilings tumbled flaming into the Greywash. The road to the front was cut.

I drifted down through smoke and fire and landed softly in what had been the depot's main thoroughfare.

There was nothing left. Where the military heart of Harren had stood minutes before, there were overlapping craters, burning wreckage, and drifting ash.

On the western side, the village was untouched. Its windows were dark, and frightened faces peered out between the shutters. Nobody came out, and nobody followed me.

I reached down and picked up the captain's head by its short grey hair. The cold blue eyes stared at nothing. It was a souvenir, a message, and proof all at once.

I dropped Battle Mode. The helm peeled away, the tendrils withdrew, and the armor sank back under my skin. I stood in the ruins looking nearly human again, apart from the head in my hand and the violet glow fading from my eyes.

Then I jumped. I cleared the crater in one bound, hit the slope of the ridge, and went up through the trees in a spray of snapped branches, landing in a crouch exactly where I'd left.

Shiryeon was still leaning against her tree with her arms crossed, watching the inferno: the craters, the burning wreckage, the severed bridges, the scattering soldiers like ants from a kicked nest. Her amber eyes moved from the destruction to me, then to the head. It was dripping on my boot.

She looked at it for a long moment.

"Thirteen minutes," she said quietly. "Two hundred soldiers. Forty mages. A captain with blessed steel. Every supply line, storehouse, and transport route destroyed. The civilian quarter untouched."

She pushed off the tree and stood straight.

"I have nothing to teach you about killing."

She turned south, toward home, then paused. "His Majesty will want a full report." Her eyes flicked to the head one more time. "I suspect you intend to deliver one."

"Let's go home," I said.

The journey back was quieter than the journey there. She navigated the dark forest by memory and moonlight, and I followed easily, my new senses painting every root and branch in sharp relief. The ancient hardwoods gave way to younger growth. Then the air turned heavier and warmer, with the faint sulfur of black earth, and the pale moonlit sky deepened to red as we crossed the border.

The black castle rose in the distance, its towers clawing at the crimson. Home, I realized. I'd started thinking of it as home.

She didn't speak again until the gates came into view.

"I've served His Majesty for two hundred years," she said. "Led campaigns. Razed fortresses. Killed heroes." A few more paces. "I have never seen a garrison dismantled that efficiently by a single combatant. Let alone one who couldn't form a dagger without strain a few days ago."

The great iron doors groaned open for her.

"I'll file my observation report by dawn," she said. She looked at the head under my arm. "I recommend you deliver yours in person."

She walked off toward the eastern wing without another word, and her footsteps faded down the dark corridor.

A small green shape shot around a corner and skidded to a stop in front of me, yellow eyes enormous.

"Master is back! Master is back already!" Skeev's eyes found the head, and his mouth fell open. "Master brought... a souvenir."

It was the first time he'd called me Master. I found I didn't mind.
