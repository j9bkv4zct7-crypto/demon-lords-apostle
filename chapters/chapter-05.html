Chapter 5

The War Table

4,636 words · about 20 min

‹ Chapter 4 Contents Chapter 6 ›

"Come," the Demon Lord said. "Look."

The map on the table showed the whole continent of Jahara in astonishing detail. The demon lands filled the northeast in black. The Celestine Empire sprawled gold across the west and center. Between them lay wide stretches of green: wilderness, villages, the Monster Glade. Carved pieces dotted the map, black for demon forces and gold for imperial armies, and a jagged front line ran through mountain passes and river crossings.

He laid one long finger on the gold.

"The goddess completed her summoning on the same day you arrived. Twenty heroes. Your former classmates." His finger slid to a point beside the imperial capital. "They are here, in Celestos, being trained, equipped, and blessed by Seraphiel Auren herself. Within weeks they will be sent to the front."

He straightened and looked at me.

"I stole you from that summoning because one apostle raised in darkness is worth twenty heroes raised in light."

The door opened behind me. Shiryeon came in, her ribs bound, moving stiffly but composed, and took her place at the table without a word.

"Still," the Demon Lord went on, "twenty heroes is not nothing. I need to know what we face." His gold eyes fixed on mine. "Tell me about your classmates. Their strengths, their weaknesses. Which of them are dangerous."

"As much as it bores me to discuss soon-to-be-dead heroes," I said, "I'll tell you everything I know."

He didn't interrupt or move. He listened like a predator committing every detail to memory.

"Souta Kirishima. Class president. A natural leader, athletic and disciplined. Whatever class he's received, he'll get the most out of it. He's the type who rallies people and coordinates attacks. He's dangerous less for raw power than because he makes everyone around him better."

The Demon Lord's finger hovered over Celestos. "Go on."

"Shiori Kanzaki." I hesitated a moment, remembering a cool glance across three rows of seats, and a face turned toward me in the light while I fell. "Quiet. Brilliant. Top of every exam since our first year. If she received anything that rewards thinking, she'll master it faster than anyone. She thinks three steps ahead. And I think she saw the light change when you took me."

That earned me a flicker of attention from the gold eyes. Shiryeon shifted slightly and listened harder.

"Gouki Tanabe. The biggest in the class. Strong, aggressive, simple, fearless. A front-line bruiser."

I went through the roster: twenty names, twenty temperaments. Who panicked under pressure and who froze. Who would sell the others out to survive, and who was genuinely selfless. I'd spent two years in the back of that lecture hall watching people instead of slides, and it turned out I had a great deal to say. I spoke for nearly half an hour.

When I finished, the war room was silent.

The Demon Lord picked up a gold piece and turned it slowly between his claws.

"Useful," he said quietly. "Very useful." He set it down. "The leader and the strategist concern me most. Without coordination, the rest are fodder." He glanced at Shiryeon. "Begin compiling countermeasures for each hero, based on the Apostle's intelligence."

She nodded.

"You have exceeded my expectations, Apostle. So I am accelerating your role." He tapped a village near the front. "Your first mission. Interested?"

"Yes," I said. "But first I have a serious question about my growth."

One golden eyebrow rose a fraction. "Speak."

I stepped up to the table and put both hands flat on the map.

"My growth rate is exceptional. But I'm level eleven, fighting level-twenty-two creatures, and nearly dying doing it. The heroes will be trained by a goddess. They'll have divine blessings, legendary equipment, and an entire empire behind them." I met his eyes. "I need more. A faster path. Is there something in this kingdom, a dungeon, an artifact, a trial, that can push me beyond what hunting can? Something that closes the gap before they reach the front?"

For a long moment he only studied me. Then he glanced at Shiryeon, and something passed between them, an unspoken exchange worn smooth by centuries. Her jaw tightened, and she gave a small shake of her head.

He ignored her.

"There is something," he said slowly. His finger traced across the map to a mountain range deep inside the demon lands, marked with a single symbol: a spiral descending into darkness. "The Abyssal Spire. An ancient dungeon beneath the Korath Mountains. Older than my reign. Older than the goddess herself, perhaps."

"My liege." Shiryeon's voice was tight. "He's level eleven."

"I am aware."

He turned back to me.

"Floor beneath floor, each deadlier than the last. No one has mapped its bottom and returned to tell of it. Those who survive emerge transformed. Those who do not are never found." The gold eyes gleamed. "Interested?"

"Very."

"Good." He straightened and folded his hands behind his back. "But not yet." His finger returned to the village. "The Spire tests the soul before it tests the body. Those below level twenty who enter are crushed by the pressure on the first floor alone. Their minds break before they take a step. Complete this mission. Reach level twenty. Then I will grant you access."

He slid something across the table toward me. It was a flat disc of obsidian carved with the same descending spiral. When I picked it up it was cold, unnaturally cold, and something inside it pulsed faintly against my palm.

"Keep it. It is the key."

I closed my hand around it.

"Now. Your mission." He tapped the village. "Harren. A human settlement near the border. Three hundred civilians. A minimal garrison, perhaps forty soldiers."

Shiryeon stepped forward, all business despite her bandages. "Our scouts report the Celestine Empire is using Harren as a forward supply depot. Weapons, provisions, and medical supplies are being funneled through it to reinforce their front-line positions."

"Destroy the supply line," the Demon Lord said. "How is your decision. Burn it, steal it, poison it." His eyes slid to me. "But understand that this will be your first encounter with humans. Not monsters. Not beasts." A pause. "Does that concern you, Apostle?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved an honest answer. I waited for some part of me to object, some voice from the life before, and nothing came.

"No," I said. "It doesn't. But before the mission, may I request three days to train and refine my abilities?"

"Three days," he repeated. He glanced at Shiryeon, who gave a slight nod. "The depot won't move. Our scouts say the next major shipment arrives in five. Three days, then. Not four. Use them well." He turned back to the wall map, dismissing me with a wave. "Shiryeon will arrange whatever you need. The eastern courtyard has been cleared for you. It is isolated, with no distractions."

She moved to the door, and I followed.

"One more thing," he said, without turning around.

I stopped.

"Level twenty is the minimum for the Spire. But the higher you enter, the deeper you will survive. Every floor you conquer grants power that cannot be obtained anywhere else in this world. Power the goddess cannot replicate. Power the heroes will never possess." The candlelight flickered across his horns. "Do not waste these three days."

In the corridor the evening light came red through the narrow windows and laid long shadows across the stone. Shiryeon walked beside me with one hand on her bound ribs.

"The eastern courtyard is reinforced stone," she said. "You can let loose without breaking anything important." At a junction she stopped and looked at me. "What's your training plan?"

"Day one, mana circulation. I force dark matter through my own channels to widen them, so I can hold more and spend less per skill."

"Painful but effective." She tilted her head. "Most demons spend months on that. You're planning to brute-force it in a day." She studied me. "Your body will resist. Dark matter isn't natural mana. It's volatile and corrosive. Force it through your channels too hard and they could rupture. I've seen warriors try it. Half ended up bedridden for weeks. The other half came out with a great deal more capacity."

She led me down a winding stair and pushed open a heavy iron door. The eastern courtyard was a wide enclosed space of black reinforced stone, perhaps sixty meters across, its walls scarred and pitted by centuries of abuse. There were no windows, only torches in high iron brackets.

"And days two and three?"

I told her. She listened and nodded slowly.

"I'll have Bris bring meals here. High-density monster meat. You'll need the calories." She turned to go, then paused, and the torchlight caught the sharp angles of her face. "One piece of advice, Apostle. When the pain becomes unbearable and your instincts tell you to stop, that's when the channels are actually widening. Don't stop."

Then she was gone, and I was alone.

I sat cross-legged at the center of the courtyard where the torchlight fell evenly from every side, laid my hands on my knees, and closed my eyes.

After one night of meditation and two days of combat, I knew the texture of the thing. It felt like liquid smoke threaded with lightning, pooled just below my sternum. Until now I'd been pushing it out through my hands. This time I pushed it inward, into the channels themselves, the invisible pathways that ran through my body like a second set of veins.

The first circuit was like swallowing ice water on an empty stomach, a sharp cold spreading through my chest and down my arms. On the second it slowed and met resistance at every junction. My channels were narrow and undeveloped, and it was like forcing a river through a straw. The third circuit brought real pain. It wasn't the clean pain of a wound but a deep, grinding ache that settled into the bone as the volatile stuff scraped along the channel walls.

I remembered what she'd said, and I didn't stop.

An hour passed. Sweat dripped off my face and darkened the stone. My jaw was clenched so hard my teeth ached. Millimeter by millimeter, the channels gave.

The pain kept climbing. My hands began to tremble.

I let it settle back into the core, and the relief was like unclenching a fist I'd been holding for an hour. When I opened my eyes, there was someone by the door.

He was a goblin, barely three feet tall, with mossy green skin, oversized ears, and a tattered brown tunic. He was clutching a broom nearly twice his height and watching me with wide yellow eyes. He plainly wasn't sure he was allowed to be there.

"You," I said.

He flinched and nearly dropped the broom. "M-me, Lord Apostle?"

"Come here."

He scurried over with the broom scraping behind him. Up close he had a crooked nose and a notched scar across one ear. He bowed so low his forehead almost touched the stone.

"Skeev is my name, Lord Apostle. Skeev sweeps the east wing. Skeev was not spying, Skeev promises. Skeev was only doing his duties and heard..."

"Relax."

He stopped talking. He didn't stop shaking.

"I have a question."

His ears perked up. "Skeev knows many things, Lord Apostle. Skeev has been in the castle forty-seven years. Ask, ask."

"Where can I find a doctor?"

"A doctor?" He scratched his scarred ear. "The castle has Surgeon Grushnak. Big orc. Wounds, broken bones, battle hurts. Very rough hands. Skeev went to him once for a stomachache and Grushnak tried to cut Skeev open." He shuddered. Then he glanced left and right and dropped his voice. "But if Lord Apostle is asking about mana channels and circulation things... there is Sereth Vaun. Wraith-kin healer in the lower west tower. She knows internal mana flow, spirit damage, all the invisible hurting things. When warlocks train too hard and crack their channels, Commander Shiryeon sends them to her." He tapped his own narrow chest. "Strange lady. Very quiet. Smells like old flowers and cold wind."

His yellow eyes went wide.

"Is Lord Apostle hurt? Skeev saw the sweating and the shaking. Skeev was worried, but Skeev did not want to interrupt, because the last person who interrupted a training apostle... well, Skeev does not actually know what an apostle does when interrupted, and Skeev did not want to find out."

"Lead me to Sereth Vaun."

His whole face lit up. "Yes, yes! Skeev will lead! Follow Skeev!"

He led me down through passages I hadn't seen yet, narrow halls with iron sconces and walls carved in old script, then a spiral stair that grew colder at every turn. "Lower west tower is where the healers and alchemists work," he chattered over his shoulder. "Most soldiers avoid it. Smells strange. She keeps jars of things Skeev does not want to know about."

Two hulking lizardmen in black armor were coming the other way. Skeev waved them aside with tremendous importance. "Make way, make way! The Apostle has business!" They exchanged a look and stepped aside.

He stopped at a tall wooden door bound in iron, its frame carved with symbols. Dried herbs and cold air drifted from behind it, the smell of winter trapped in a sealed room. He knocked three times with a tiny fist.

"Enter," said a voice, soft and papery.

Skeev pushed the door open and bowed me through. "Skeev will wait here, Lord Apostle. Skeev does not like the jars."

The room was round and dim. Shelves lined every wall, filled with glass jars of luminous liquid, dried organs, floating eyes, and crystals I couldn't name. Bundles of pale herbs hung from the ceiling, and a low fire burned blue-green in a stone hearth.

The woman at the long worktable was very tall and impossibly thin, close to six feet. Her skin was a pale, corpse-like grey and faintly translucent, so that I could almost see the dark veins beneath. Her hair fell straight and bone-white past her waist. Her eyes were solid black, with no iris and no white, only depthless dark in a narrow, angular face. She wore layered robes of violet and charcoal, and her long, delicate fingers held a vial of something glowing amber.

"The Apostle," she said. It wasn't a question. Her voice was quiet and measured, like wind through an empty room. "Your mana channels are inflamed. I can feel it from here. Sit."

She gestured to a stone chair by the hearth.

"Thank you for the observation," I said, "but I'm not here for a checkup. I have a theory, and I need your intelligence for it."

She paused. The black eyes regarded me with something that might have been curiosity, though her face gave almost nothing away.

"A theory." She folded her arms into her sleeves and leaned back against the table, and white hair spilled over one shoulder. "I am not accustomed to being consulted on theory. Warriors come to me broken. I put them back together. That is the arrangement." The fire crackled. "But you are not an ordinary warrior, are you. Speak. I will listen."

Something in a jar near the ceiling blinked at me.

"Mana potions are slow," I said. "You drink one, it goes through digestion, enters the bloodstream, and only then gets filtered into the channels. I learned that today the hard way, twice in a row. I think there's a way to fuse blood and mana potion together, so that mana regenerates almost instantly."

Her black eyes widened, very slightly. It was the first real reaction I'd seen from her. She unfolded her arms and laid her palms flat on the table behind her.

"Interesting," she whispered.

She began to pace along the shelves, one finger trailing across the glass, and took down two vessels, one of thick crimson and one of shimmering blue.

"Blood and mana potion. Fundamentally incompatible in their raw forms. You want to bypass absorption entirely. Infuse the potion directly into the blood, so the mana travels with every heartbeat. The blood becomes the delivery mechanism." She set them side by side. "The principle is sound. But there are two problems. First, mana potions are alchemically volatile. Mixed improperly with blood, they cause coagulation. The blood clots, and the subject dies within minutes." A second finger rose. "Second: whose blood? Every species is different. What works for an orc would kill a human instantly." She leaned forward slightly. "But you have considered these problems already, haven't you? Otherwise you would not be standing in my tower."

"I can manipulate dark matter," I said. "What if I infused it into my own genetic code, at a minuscule scale? It would act as the barrier between the blood and the potion. It wouldn't mix them. It would hold them together by force, in forced coexistence."

She went very still. In the jar near the ceiling, the blinking thing stopped blinking.

Then she moved. She swept a clutter of instruments aside with one arm, pulled a sheet of pale parchment from a drawer, and picked up a charcoal stylus.

"Say that again," she said. "Slowly."

So I did. I described dark matter worked at a microscopic level and woven into my blood cells themselves, a membrane that would keep the potion's compounds and the blood apart cell by cell, so they never touched and never clotted, while mana passed through the membrane as if it were a semi-permeable wall. I'd sat through a semester of cell biology as a required elective and hated every minute of it. I was grateful for it now.

Her hand flew across the parchment as I talked, drawing circles within circles and molecular structures I half recognized.

"A trilayer system," she murmured. "Blood. Dark-matter membrane. Mana compound. Each cell becomes its own delivery vessel." She looked up. "Theoretically elegant. Potentially revolutionary." The black eyes narrowed. "Also potentially fatal. You would be asking your dark matter to work on a scale smaller than the eye can see, continuously, inside your own body. One lapse, one moment of lost focus, and the membrane fails. The potion touches raw blood." She drew a single line through the diagram. "Instant clotting. Cardiac arrest. Death." She set the stylus down. "How fine is your control, Apostle? Honestly."

"Not fine enough yet. But I'm working on that. The more familiar I become with the power, the less it costs."

She drummed her long fingers on the table, then picked up the stylus again and began writing in the margins.

"Your logic is not flawed. Familiarity breeds efficiency. If you knew your dark matter intimately enough at the cellular scale, the membrane could become autonomous. A passive function, not an active one." She sketched a curve sloping downward: cost falling over time. "Like breathing. You do not think about breathing." She put the stylus down and turned to face me fully. "Under normal circumstances, this would take months. Perhaps years." A pause. "But you are not normal circumstances."

From a shelf she took a small glass case. Inside lay a thin needle of black crystal.

"A mana resonance needle. Inserted beneath the skin, it amplifies internal mana perception tenfold. Healers use it to diagnose channel damage. But in theory, if you used it during circulation, you would perceive your dark matter at the cellular level. You could see it, and feel its individual particles. It would accelerate your fine control dramatically." The black eyes fixed on me. "It is also extraordinarily painful, and I would need to supervise. Shall I?"

"I'd like to begin immediately," I said. "I have three days."

Something shifted in her expression, the faintest trace of what might have been respect.

"Three days," she murmured. "Ambitious."

She prepared quickly: a cloth draped over the stone chair, a vial of translucent paste, a strip of dark leather, a bowl of luminous green liquid. "Remove your shirt and sit," she said without looking up.

The chair was cold against my back, and the blue-green fire gave almost no warmth. Up close, her translucent skin was unsettling. I could see dark veins pulsing in her cheeks, her throat, and her hands. She smelled exactly as Skeev had said, of old flowers and cold wind.

She dipped a finger in the paste and drew a small circle over my sternum. "A numbing agent. It will dull the insertion. After that..." A pause. "It will not help."

She held up the needle. It was about four inches long and impossibly thin, and it seemed to swallow the light around it.

"It will lodge between your second and third rib, beside your mana core. Once it activates, you will feel every channel in your body. Every cell. Every particle of dark matter. The human body was not built to perceive itself at that resolution. Your mind will read the overload as extreme pain. Most patients lose consciousness within thirty seconds." She positioned the point against my chest. "Ready, Apostle?"

I let out a long breath and made my mind go flat and still, the way I used to before an exam I knew I could ace.

"Ready," I said. "I've always believed people die because they panic instead of reacting. So I don't panic."

She nodded once. "Good."

She pushed the needle in.

There was pressure, then a cold, sliding feeling as the crystal slipped between my ribs, like a splinter of winter lodged in my chest.

Then she twisted it, and the world detonated inward.

My perception collapsed to a pinpoint, then exploded outward in every direction at once, but inward, into me. I could feel everything. Every mana channel, not as vague warmth anymore but as thousands of distinct pathways branching like the roots of an ancient tree through muscle, bone, and organ, glowing violet behind my closed eyes.

And deeper. I could feel my cells. There were millions of them, billions, each one a tiny pulsing universe. Scattered among them like black stars were particles of dark matter. Mine. They were nested inside me at a scale I'd never imagined.

Then the pain arrived.

It came like a wall of white fire through every nerve I had. My brain had never been meant to take in that much information at once. My fingers dug into the stone armrests, my jaw locked, and the veins stood out on my neck.

Sereth Vaun watched me clinically.

Ten seconds. Twenty. Thirty.

My vision blurred. The edges of my consciousness flickered. My whole body begged to pass out.

I breathed. I let the pain move through me the way weather moves across a sky. It existed, and I acknowledged it. I didn't let it own me.

Forty seconds.

Her black eyes widened. "Still conscious," she whispered. "Remarkable."

The pain didn't lessen, but my mind steadied around it. Inside the agony, I began to see the dark matter with perfect, crystalline clarity.

I reached for the particles. I didn't use my hands or my mana. I used intent. One answered, then another, then ten, then a hundred. I didn't force them. I invited them, as if this connection had always been meant to exist, as if I were flexing a muscle I'd been born with and never found. They began to move together in a slow, synchronized orbit inside a single blood cell, settling against its membrane, not merging and not destroying, simply coexisting.

Sereth Vaun laid two fingers on my wrist and closed her eyes.

"Your dark matter is responding at the cellular level," she said quietly. The clinical detachment in her voice had cracked. "I have never seen this. Not once in three hundred years of practice."

One hour passed, then two. The pain changed from a roar to a constant hum as my mind adapted. I memorized everything: the texture of individual particles, the way they vibrated when I called them, the rhythm of moving them together.

At some point Bris appeared in the doorway with a tray. Skeev whispered something to her. She peeked in, saw the state I was in, set the tray down without a sound, and backed away.

Four hours.

"Your mana is at about forty percent," Sereth Vaun said. "You should stop."

"I'll rest," I managed, "and come back tomorrow."

I let go. The synchronized particles slowed and drifted apart, and the amplified sense dimmed as I pulled my awareness back to normal scale. It was like going from seeing every grain of sand on a beach to seeing only the shoreline.

"Brace yourself," she said. "Removal is unpleasant."

It was. The crystal slid out with a wet, cold sensation that turned my stomach. A single drop of dark blood welled up, and she pressed a cloth soaked in the green liquid against it. It stung, then went numb.

"Four hours in your first session. Most do not last one." She studied me, and there was something new in the black eyes. It wasn't warmth. It was professional fascination. "Your synchronization was crude but functional. Two more sessions of equal length, and you may establish a foundation that persists on its own." She folded her arms into her sleeves. "Return tomorrow morning. Rest tonight. Eat heavily. And, Apostle..."

I looked at her.

"If this trilayer theory works, it changes everything we understand about potion absorption. Not only for you. For every soldier in His Majesty's army." She turned back to her table. "Do not die before we finish."

Skeev scrambled up as I came out, his broom clattering. "Lord Apostle looks terrible! Skeev will carry the food tray to your quarters!"

He balanced Bris's tray on his head with surprising strength for something his size.

"Thank you, Skeev. On the way, would you take a message to Commander Shiryeon?"

"A message!" His ears swiveled. "Skeev is excellent at messages! Very reliable! Skeev has lost only one message in forty-seven years, and that was because Skeev was chased by a hellhound, so it was not Skeev's fault!" Then the name caught up with him, and his yellow eyes went wide. "To... the Commander. Skeev is brave. The Commander has only threatened to skin Skeev alive twice this month, so the odds are improving." He looked up at me expectantly, the tray wobbling. "What message?"

"Ask her how her injuries are healing," I said. "And tell her I hope her day was well."

Skeev stopped dead, and the tray nearly went over.

"Ask Commander Shiryeon... how her injuries are healing?" He stared at me. "And... hope her day was well?"

His mouth opened, then closed, then opened again.

"Lord Apostle wants Skeev to deliver... kind words... to Commander Shiryeon." His voice dropped to a whisper. "The Commander Shiryeon. The one who beheads things. The one who sleeps with a blade under her pillow. The one who once threw a troll off the ramparts for looking at her wrong."

He swallowed hard.

"Skeev will deliver this message. Skeev is brave. Skeev is loyal."

He didn't sound convinced of either.

At my door he set the tray down with trembling hands and backed toward the corridor. "Skeev will go now. Skeev will find the Commander. Skeev will say the words exactly. Skeev will probably survive."

He disappeared, muttering, his broom scraping behind him.

I ate slowly: tender roasted meat, dense dark bread, and a thick stew that warmed me from the inside. My channels throbbed. But underneath the ache there was something new, a faint awareness of the dark matter particles inside me. It was distant and fading, but it was there.

The connection hadn't vanished when the needle came out.

I lay back on the bed, and sleep took me before I could finish the thought.
