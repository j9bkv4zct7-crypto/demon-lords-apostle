Chapter 6

A Demon, Finally

4,429 words · about 19 min

‹ Chapter 5 Contents Chapter 7 ›

Skeev woke me with breakfast and a report.

"Lord Apostle! Skeev brings food! And Skeev is alive!"

He set the tray down: roasted meat, a thick slab of dark bread, a bowl of spiced marrow broth, and a single blue mana potion. Then he cleared his throat, squared his narrow shoulders, and did his best impression of Shiryeon. On a three-foot goblin it was magnificent.

"The Commander said, 'Tell the Apostle my ribs are none of his concern. And if he has the energy to send pleasantries through goblins, he has the energy to train harder.'"

He let the posture go and grinned with every crooked tooth.

"But Skeev noticed something. When Skeev said the words, the Commander's ear twitched. Skeev has been in this castle forty-seven years. Skeev knows what ear twitches mean." He leaned in. "She was not angry, Lord Apostle." One yellow eye winked. "Skeev thinks she was surprised."

I ate fast and drank the potion. Then I went back down to the tower that smelled of winter.

Sereth Vaun had been busy. New diagrams were pinned across her wall, cell structures annotated in a tight, precise hand, with my dark matter drawn in as tiny black stars. The needle lay ready in its case.

"You still carry residual synchronization from yesterday," she said the moment I walked in. "Good. The connection is taking root. Today we push deeper. Yesterday you moved particles one at a time, like counting grains of sand. Today you reach for clusters. Fifty. Then a hundred. Then a thousand, commanded as a single unit." The black eyes met mine. "The strain will be much greater. If you feel your consciousness fragmenting, not fading but fragmenting, tell me at once. There is a difference between passing out and having your mind shatter."

The cold slid between my ribs. It was familiar now. She twisted the needle, and the world collapsed inward again.

I let the pain break over me like a wave on rock and reached for fifty.

It was like closing a fist around a handful of fireflies. Every particle vibrated at its own frequency, and forcing them into unison was like asking fifty different hearts to beat as one. Ten aligned, then twenty, then thirty-one. My temples throbbed and my mana poured out like water through a cracked jar. Forty-five. Fifty.

They locked together.

"Fifty," Sereth Vaun whispered, fingers on my wrist. "Hold it. Now another fifty."

Splitting my attention was like holding two separate things in two separate hands, each trying to wriggle free. Eighty. Ninety. A hundred. Then two hundred, three hundred, and with every step the clusters felt less like groups and more like extensions of me, fingers on a hand I was only now discovering I had.

At five hundred, my mana dropped below sixty percent.

At a thousand, I hit a wall.

My vision didn't blur. It fractured, like looking through a shattered mirror, and the edges of my consciousness began to splinter.

I stopped pushing. I breathed slowly through my nose. I didn't chase the thousand. I held the five hundred I had and let them orbit, and I listened to the rhythm of five hundred particles moving as a single organism. One by one the shards of my vision clicked back into place.

"You found the boundary," Sereth Vaun said quietly, "and you did not cross it. That is worth more than breaking through would have been." She was already sketching a large circle full of smaller circles, each full of dots. "A thousand requires a different way of thinking. You are holding five hundred separate connections. That is why you hit the wall. You need hierarchy. Not a thousand individuals, but ten groups of a hundred that keep themselves in order." She set the stylus down. "Your mana is at thirty-five percent. We stop here. Tomorrow we implement the hierarchy."

"No," I said. "We don't stop."

Her hand froze on the needle.

"The heroes won't give up," I said, "so neither will I. Bring me mana potions. And any buff you have for mental concentration and durability. Cast all of it."

She looked at me for several long seconds. Something moved behind the black eyes. Understanding, perhaps, or recognition of something she hadn't seen in a very long time.

"The heroes," she repeated softly. Then she went to her shelves.

A blue mana potion pushed my reserves back up to something workable. A silver vial followed: "Cerebral Clarity. Distilled from mindworm glands and frost lotus. Two hours of sharpened thought, then a migraine you will remember." It tasted like liquid metal and ice, and the world snapped into crystal focus as if fog had burned off a window. A third vial, amber, she set aside. "Neural stabilizer. Emergency only. If you begin to fragment again, I give you this, and you sleep for twelve hours whether you like it or not."

She rolled back her sleeves and held both palms above my temples, and violet light poured into my skull and settled around my mind like scaffolding. "A fortified mental barrier. It will not prevent damage. It buys you about three seconds before fragmentation becomes permanent." Her voice was steady. Her eyes weren't, quite. "I have done everything I can to keep your mind intact. Now. Five groups of one hundred. Give each an identity, a color, a feeling. You are no longer holding five hundred. You are holding five."

I closed my eyes. Through the tonic, every particle burned sharp and distinct.

And I realized I didn't want to simply hold them.

A thousand moving in synchrony was a parlor trick. I'd come down here with a theory, and the theory needed every cell in my body rewritten. I'd never get there by holding things. I had to build something that ran on its own, the way a program runs once you've written it.

So I wrote one.

The first hundred I painted deep crimson and gave a single purpose: convert. Find an unconverted blood cell, open its membrane, and weave dark matter into its DNA. A converted cell would begin producing dark matter of its own, and those new particles would join the crimson group and repeat the process. The second hundred, amber, got the same order for a different territory: my chest, my heart, my lungs. Between them they made a self-replicating system, a virus of evolution rewriting my biology from the inside out.

The last three hundred weren't workers. They were the architecture. Cold blue mapped every converted cell and tracked the spread across a living grid, so there was no overlap and nothing was wasted. White-silver followed behind, compressing each new cell and tightening its molecular bonds until it was denser and harder than the one before. Black-violet was the engine: it took in feedback from all the others, found the patterns, and rewrote the converters' instructions in real time, so every cycle ran faster than the last.

Five clusters, five autonomous legions. I let them go.

My body ignited.

It wasn't pain, exactly. It was heat and pressure, the feeling of being edited at the molecular level. Cells converted in chains, one into two, two into four, four into sixteen. Violet-black lines crept up my forearms, across my chest, along my neck.

"Apostle..." Sereth Vaun grabbed a monitoring crystal and held it to my chest. It pulsed violet, faster and faster. "This is exponential. You've built a self-propagating system. Fifteen percent of your blood converted. Twenty. Twenty-eight."

It still wasn't enough. Converted blood was only half the theory.

I layered a second order on top of the three architects: integrate. In an ordinary body, mana and blood run side by side and never meet, two rivers in parallel. Cold blue found the stabilized cells and mapped the channels beside them. White-silver began opening tiny controlled pores in the wall between channel and vessel, perforating it without destroying it. Black-violet metered every exchange point. Too much mana in the blood would rupture the cells. Too much dark matter in the channels would corrode them. It found the balance and kept recalibrating.

The first junction opened in my left hand.

Mana and blood became one substance, a hybrid fluid that carried both kinds of energy at once. My fingers tingled. The skin on the back of my hand darkened toward ash.

Sereth Vaun dropped the crystal. She snatched it back up, and it was strobing so fast between violet and blue that the colors blurred into one churning light.

"You are merging your mana system and your circulatory system into a single network," she whispered. "Every healer and biomancer in three thousand years of recorded magic has called this impossible. The rejection rate alone..." Her hands were shaking as she checked my pulse. "Forty-four percent conversion. Twelve percent integration. No rejection. No necrosis."

The junctions cascaded up my arm, across my shoulder, and down my chest, each one a tiny reactor. My regeneration spiked. Mana no longer trickled through narrow channels. It surged through my whole circulatory system with every heartbeat. The container wasn't just refilling. It was getting bigger.

Fifty percent. Twenty percent integration. Steam began curling off my skin.

Then the fluid reached my bone marrow, and the real pain arrived.

It was physical and bone-deep. My skeleton resonated with the new fluid as if it were being hollowed out and refilled with molten glass. I felt hairline fractures spidering through my ribs. Human bone had never been designed to carry this.

Sereth Vaun reached for the amber vial.

"Don't," I said.

She froze.

"I need to integrate my bones." Steam rose off my shoulders. "A strong foundation, or this body won't carry the power or the weight. Do you have dragon blood? Anything from a powerful creature?"

She set the vial down slowly. The fear in her face didn't leave, but something rose beside it: that same fascination, ten times stronger.

"An external catalyst," she said. "Blood dense with primal mana, to reinforce the skeleton during conversion so it doesn't shatter under the load." She was already moving.

From a cabinet behind three runic seals she took out a small chest. Four vials lay inside on black velvet. She went down them briskly. Wyvern was potent but diluted. Basilisk would calcify rather than integrate. Elder troll was regenerative but incompatible with dark matter. She lifted the fourth. It held barely two fingers of liquid, black and flecked with gold that swirled like captured starlight.

"Abyssal Drake. From a creature His Majesty killed with his own hands two hundred years ago. I have kept it for centuries, waiting for a use worthy of it." The gold flecks pulsed to a rhythm of their own. "Abyssal Drakes are born in the space between dimensions. Their blood carries dimensional mana, denser and heavier than anything biological. It would bond with your hybrid fluid and rebuild your skeleton at the molecular level." Her black eyes held mine. "It will destroy your bones and remake them from the inside out. There is no precedent. If it fails, your skeleton liquefies."

Fifty-five percent. My ribs creaked. Either the bones adapted, or they broke.

I took the vial. It was unnaturally cold, and the gold flecks quickened as they felt the dark matter in my hand.

"Healing magic," I said. "Slow and continuous. Don't fix what breaks. Just keep the tissue alive long enough for this to take hold."

She hesitated for exactly one second. "Understood."

She stood behind the chair, and soft green light bloomed from her palms, a bare whisper of restoration. I uncorked the vial. It smelled of ozone and iron and something older than this world's name. I drank.

It tasted like swallowing a thunderstorm.

I pulled a hundred particles from the converters, which had multiplied enough by then to run without them, and built a sixth cluster painted gold to match the blood. Bone integration. Merge the drake blood with the hybrid fluid, target the skeleton only, start at the feet, follow the conversion wave upward, and rebuild everything.

The blood didn't flow. It crawled, heavy and deliberate. When it reached my feet, the first metatarsal split lengthwise, and black-gold poured into the marrow like molten metal into a mold. My toes shattered and re-formed. When they were done, the bones weren't white anymore. They were black, veined with gold, and far denser.

It climbed my body. My ankles splintered and reknit. Both shins cracked at once with a sound like twin gunshots off the stone walls. My kneecaps came apart and reassembled thicker, latticed with dark matter. My femurs split slowly, grinding, and I felt every millimeter as the blood filled them like concrete poured into pillars.

"Pelvis," Sereth Vaun warned, her voice strained. "Then the spine. Apostle, if a single vertebra shifts during reconstruction, you will never walk again."

So I acted before it got there.

I drew dark matter out of my own bloodstream. It was fluid and dense and answered like a limb, and I pushed it out through my pores to harden over my skin in sections: legs, hips, abdomen, ribs. It formed a cast, an external skeleton, black and glossy, that held everything in alignment while the bones inside it broke. Maintaining it on top of six internal clusters made my mana plunge, and only the tonic kept my mind from buckling.

"Brilliant," she breathed. "Lock the vertebral column. Not a millimeter."

I clamped the cast around my spine until every vertebra was held in its own cell.

L5 cracked, then L4 and L3. Each split, flooded with black-gold, and rebuilt itself in place. The thoracic spine went faster, because the engine had learned the pattern: crack, flood, rebuild. My ribs went all at once, twenty-four bones tearing open like a zipper, and Sereth Vaun's green light flared as she fought to keep my lungs inflated.

"Seventy-eight percent conversion," she gasped. "Fifty-one integration. Skeleton at seventy."

Then the neck, C7 to C1, the only bridge between my brain and the rest of me. I poured everything into the cast at my throat and compressed it to diamond hardness.

C1, the atlas, cracked.

For two heartbeats the world was silent. It wasn't quiet. Sound simply ceased to exist. My vision whited out, and the cast screamed under the pressure and flexed toward breaking.

I held.

Gold stitched the atlas back together like thread through broken porcelain. Sound came back, then sight. Last of all came my skull: microfractures spiderwebbing across every plate at once, drake blood seeping through them like roots through stone, my jaw unhinging and resetting with an audible click.

Then it was over.

"Skeletal reconstruction," Sereth Vaun whispered, "one hundred percent."

She sank into a chair. Her green light went out, and her hands shook violently.

"What are you becoming, Apostle?"

I let the cast go. It didn't crumble. It flowed back in through my pores, reabsorbed without waste.

"A demon," I said. "Finally."

My skin had changed. It was still recognizably mine, but darker now, an ashen undertone laced with faint violet-black veins like ink drawn under parchment. My hands felt heavier when I flexed them, every knuckle reinforced with bone that hummed.

I stood up, and the stone floor cracked under my heels.

The cracks spread quietly in a spiderweb. I weighed considerably more now. My skeleton was denser than anything that grows naturally, and my muscles hadn't caught up with it yet. But I was standing.

Sereth Vaun stared at the monitoring crystal, set it down, and picked it up again. "Ninety-one percent conversion. Seventy-three percent integration. Your mana capacity has tripled. The hybrid system is using your entire body as a reservoir. You don't have mana channels anymore, Apostle. You have a mana body."

The door creaked. Skeev's enormous eyes appeared in the gap, took in the dark skin, the glowing veins, and the cracked floor, and his jaw dropped.

"L-Lord Apostle is... Lord Apostle is different."

Then the air pressure in the room changed. A shadow fell across the doorway, and Skeev squeaked and scrambled out of the way.

The Demon Lord stepped inside, ducking under the lintel.

His gold eyes took me in from head to foot in a single measured glance. His expression didn't change, but something in those eyes sharpened. It was a predator noticing something it hadn't expected to find.

"You've been busy," he said.

He crossed the room, took in Sereth Vaun's trembling hands, the scattered vials, and the diagrams, and stopped three feet from me. Then he did something I didn't expect. He breathed in slowly through his nose, scenting me.

"Abyssal Drake blood," he said quietly. "My kill. Two centuries ago."

Sereth Vaun straightened in her chair. "My lord, the Apostle requested..."

"I didn't ask for an explanation."

She went silent.

"Show me your hand."

I held it out, palm up. He took my wrist. His grip was cold and absolute, like being held by stone that had decided to move. I felt his awareness press against mine, and it wasn't mana or magic. It was something older. He held me for ten seconds.

Then he squeezed.

It would have powdered a human wrist. For him it was casual. My new radius and ulna took the load, black lattice humming, gold flaring through the bone. They held. My skin didn't even bruise.

A faint curve came to the corner of his mouth. It wasn't quite a smile. Something adjacent.

He let go.

"Day two," he said, "and you have already begun rewriting your species."

He went to the wall and studied the diagrams: the cluster hierarchies, the integration notes.

"Sereth. Document everything. Every measurement, every variable, every result. What the Apostle is doing has never been done. When this war ends, I want a complete record." He looked back at me over his shoulder. "Your capacity has tripled. Your skeleton surpasses most mid-tier demons. My scholars will need a new word for whatever your cells are becoming." He turned to face me fully. "And you are still level eleven."

He didn't say it as a criticism. If this was eleven, I could see him wondering what twenty would look like. Or fifty, or a hundred.

"Harren stands. You have one day left. I meant to send Shiryeon with you as insurance." The gold eyes held mine. "I am reconsidering whether you need insurance. Do you want her with you, or do you go alone?"

In the doorway, Skeev had stopped breathing.

"I'd like her to come," I said. "As your eyes, my liege, to report back to you. And as my teacher, on my first mission."

"My eyes," he repeated. Amusement touched the words, so faintly that it was gone almost before it arrived. "And your teacher." He turned to the door. "Practical. Humble. A dangerous combination." At the threshold, as Skeev flattened himself against the wall, he paused. "I will inform her. You leave at dawn. Use your remaining time well, Apostle. The humans at Harren will not care how elegant your biology is. They will care how fast you kill them."

He left, and the air pressure went back to normal. Skeev slid down the wall onto the floor.

"Skeev's heart almost stopped. Skeev is too small for this much tension."

Sereth Vaun was already at her table, uncorking ink with hands that still shook. "Your conversion is still running without your input. By morning you should be fully converted and nearly fully integrated. Your muscle will thicken to match your bones, and your organs will adapt. It will be uncomfortable. Possibly very uncomfortable." She looked over her shoulder. "Eat. Heavily. Protein, fat, marrow. If you don't feed the process, it will begin eating your muscle for raw material."

Skeev bounced up. "Food? Skeev can get food! Meat and bones and marrow soup and black bread and... Skeev will bring a mountain!" He was gone, bare feet slapping down the corridor.

Sereth Vaun set down her quill.

"Apostle. One thing." Her black eyes held mine. "What you built inside yourself today, the self-replicating clusters, the three-tier support structure, the bone protocol with a foreign catalyst, was not my design. I gave you tools. The architecture was entirely yours." She tilted her head. "I have lived four hundred and twelve years. I have treated demon generals, elder liches, ancient wyrms. I have never seen a mind that works the way yours does. You do not learn magic." She picked up the quill again. "You engineer it. Go and eat. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow you go to war."

I went back to my room trying to walk lightly. Each step left a faint print in the stone anyway. A pair of demon guards did a double take at my skin.

My bed frame groaned when I sat on it. Skeev burst in a minute later with a tray almost as big as he was: charred meat, split marrow bones still steaming, black bread, an iron-smelling stew, and three more mana potions. "Skeev stole extra marrow from the ogre captain's portion! He will not notice." A pause. "Probably."

I was starving in a way I'd never known, a hollow, furious demand from somewhere below appetite. I cracked a marrow bone with my bare fingers. It snapped like a dry twig, and I drank it straight.

"Skeev. I need someone who knows this castle. Every corridor, every room."

"Skeev knows every corridor! Even the secret ones that aren't really secret, because Skeev is very small and very sneaky!" He tapped his crooked nose. "But if Lord Apostle wants someone more... official..."

He stuck his head into the corridor and let out a rapid, chittering call. A minute later measured footsteps approached, and a tall demoness appeared in the doorway. She had slate-blue skin, white hair cropped close to her skull, and pale amber eyes. A thin scar ran from her left temple to her jaw. Her black uniform had silver trim.

She bowed crisply. "Thalia Grey. Head of household staff, Lord Apostle. How may I serve you?"

Her eyes took in the cracked floor, my darkened skin, and the glowing veins, and her expression didn't change at all. I liked her immediately.

"I'd like incense," I said. "Something to burn while I rest."

A small leather notebook appeared in her hand. "Blackroot sage: earthy and heavy, favored by officers for meditation. Ember lotus: warm and slightly sweet, for deep sleep. Void ash: cold and sharp, it speeds mana recovery during rest." Her gaze flicked to my veins. "In your state I would recommend the void ash. If comfort matters more than function, the ember lotus."

"Skeev likes the ember lotus!" Skeev bounced on his toes. "It smells like warm things! Not like the void ash, which smells like the inside of a dead glacier!"

Thalia Grey didn't acknowledge him.

"One of each," I said.

She returned in exactly ten minutes with three ceramic burners and three labeled pouches. "Light them separately or together. I would advise against burning void ash and blackroot sage at the same time. In non-demon physiology the combination can cause vivid hallucinations." She paused. "Will there be anything else, Lord Apostle?"

"Thank you, Thalia," I said. "You may go, beautiful."

Her eyes widened by a fraction. The scar at her temple twitched, and a faint flush of darker blue rose in her cheeks. She recovered in half a second.

"...You are welcome, Lord Apostle."

She bowed and left, a little faster than she'd arrived.

Skeev stared at me from the floor with his mouth open. Then an enormous grin split his face. "Lord Apostle just made Thalia Grey blush. Thalia Grey once made an ogre captain cry for folding linens wrong." He slapped his knee. "Skeev has witnessed history."

I lit the ember lotus with a flick of dark matter from my fingertip, then the void ash, and warm and cold smoke braided together in the air. My mana quickened, and the tension in my rebuilt muscles began to ease.

"Should Skeev stay, Lord Apostle?"

"Stand guard at the door, Skeev. I'm going to bed."

He puffed out his chest. "Skeev will guard with his life! Nothing gets past Skeev! Not demons, not soldiers, not Thalia Grey with her scary notebook!" A stool scraped in the corridor, and the door closed. "Skeev is on duty," came the muffled announcement.

I lay back. I had one more idea before sleep, because I don't know how to waste a night.

I didn't summon the dark matter. I let it rise, generated now by the clusters in my own tissue, and pushed it out through my pores into a thin obsidian layer over my skin. Then I shaped it. Gauntlets formed first and flexed with my fingers. A breastplate molded to my new ribs, followed by shoulders and shins, each segment interlocking and articulated so it moved with me rather than against me. I grew a blade from the forearm guard, three inches, six, a foot, and drew it back in. I reshaped it into a hook and drew it back. I raised spikes along my knuckles and pulled them in again.

It wasn't separate from me. It was me, an extension of the dark matter already woven into every cell. It cost nothing but circulation.

The ember lotus pulled me down toward sleep, and the void ash kept my channels open, and I drifted into a strange middle state, my body resting while the dark matter kept learning. I dreamed in geometry, in pressure gradients, in the mathematics of force across articulated plates. Somewhere in the deep hours the armor shifted on its own. My sleeping clusters had solved a problem I hadn't consciously noticed. The shoulder plates redistributed and overlapped for full range of motion, and fine channels opened through the gauntlets so mana could reach my fingertips even while armored.

At some point I heard Skeev snoring outside the door.

When I opened my eyes, red light was bleeding through the window, and the armor lay on me like a second skin, black and seamless, violet light pulsing faintly at the joints. I checked the numbers once. Conversion complete. Integration at ninety-seven percent. My mana pool was well over twice what it had been yesterday morning. Eight autonomous clusters were running now, three of them grown in my sleep.

Still level eleven. The recommended level for Harren was twenty.

A knock came at the door, sharp and deliberate. It wasn't Skeev.

"Apostle," said a cold, precise voice. "Dawn has arrived."

"I'm ready," I said.
