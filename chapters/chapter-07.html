Chapter 7

The Road to Harren

2,996 words · about 13 min

‹ Chapter 6 Contents Chapter 8 ›

When I opened the door, Shiryeon was waiting in full armor. This time it wasn't leather. It was dark segmented plate that fit her as if it had been grown instead of forged, with a plain curved sword on her hip beside the daggers. Her ash-grey skin caught the torchlight, and her amber eyes swept over me and stopped.

The exoskeleton covered me from head to foot, black and organic, violet veins pulsing faintly at every joint. My frame was bigger than it had been two days earlier, and denser. The armor moved a little with each breath, like something alive.

Her expression didn't change, but she didn't move for three full seconds. It was the most surprise I'd ever seen from her.

"You look different," she said flatly.

Behind her, Skeev was slumped on his stool against the wall, snoring, a thread of drool running from his chin to his chest. She stepped over his feet without looking down.

"We travel light," she said, already walking. "No caravan, no escort. It's two days to Harren through the borderlands, or one if we cut through the Glade. I prefer the Glade. It's faster and more dangerous. And you need levels." She stopped at the end of the corridor and turned. "Rules. You follow my lead on navigation. You handle your own fights unless I decide you're about to die. I won't coddle you. His Majesty ordered me to observe and mentor, not babysit." She pushed open the gate. Cold air rushed in under the red sky. "Questions?"

"None."

I stepped out into the courtyard and breathed in, and I felt every fiber of the armor as I did it. The wind broke across its surface, and I felt that too, as if the armor had nerve endings of its own. My regeneration was constant now, a river that never stopped. I could already feel the excess pooling with nowhere to go.

Wasting it offended me, so I gave it a job.

I took Cluster Six and rewrote its orders: intercept the overflow, route it to the outer layer of the armor, compress it into a standing barrier, and keep it fed forever. A thin membrane of pressure built across the plates, thickened, and held.

Ten paces ahead, Shiryeon stopped and turned. "Your armor just changed density."

It wasn't a question. She'd felt the mana shift.

"Interesting," she said, and walked on.

As long as I regenerated, the barrier would never run dry. It was a passive shield that cost me nothing.

The two tusked sentries at the perimeter gate, twelve feet tall in black iron, watched us pass. One grunted to the other in low demonic, and I understood it perfectly.

That's the Apostle.

Shiryeon set a punishing pace, and for two hours she didn't say a word. Black rock gave way to grey stone, grey stone to brown dirt, and the red sky faded to a dull overcast. Then green rose in front of us like a wall. She drew the curved sword at the tree line.

"From here, everything wants to kill you."

Four feral goblins came screeching out of the undergrowth before we'd gone thirty meters. They were wild-eyed and hunched, carrying bone knives, nothing like Skeev. Shiryeon sheathed her blade.

"Yours. Show me what two days bought you."

"These weaklings are a waste of time," I said.

I didn't raise a hand. I simply pushed: raw intent, the will to kill, broadcast through the armor and the barrier, with the clusters amplifying it like an antenna. The air warped.

The first goblin's legs folded. The second skidded to a stop with foam at the corners of its mouth. The third collapsed and curled into a ball. The fourth turned to run, made two steps, and crawled, then lay flat and shook.

None of them were dead. They were simply broken, overloaded by a predator so far above them that their instincts had shut down.

Shiryeon looked at the twitching goblins for a long moment.

"Impressive display." Her voice was flat. "Useless. They're alive. Alive means no experience. You're eleven and you need twenty. Intimidation doesn't close that gap." Something large crashed through the trees farther in. "Save the theatrics for Harren. You need kills. Real ones. Things that fight back."

She was right, which was irritating. So I took the same trick and turned it inside out.

I planted my feet and let the killing intent pour out of me in every direction. It wasn't focused this time. It was a pulse, amplified through all eight clusters, a signal fire lit in the dark. It soaked into the soil and rippled through the canopy.

For three seconds every bird and every insect in the Glade went silent.

Then the noise came back louder and angrier.

Shiryeon's hand went to her sword. "You just rang the dinner bell for everything within half a mile." She didn't sound worried. She sounded interested. "Bold. Stupid. But bold."

A razorback boar the size of a small horse came out of the brush on the left, tusks like curved swords. Two dire wolves fanned wide to the right, moving in coordinated silence. Something dropped from the canopy above me: a canopy stalker, six-limbed and reptilian, its chameleon skin still shifting from bark brown to mottled green as it fell toward my head.

"You called them," Shiryeon said. "You kill them."

I didn't dodge.

Needles burst from every surface of my armor at once, hundreds of them, three inches long and compressed to maximum density, flying out in a sphere. Mid-flight each one sprouted tiny hooked fins that spun it into a corkscrew, so they bored in rather than simply stabbing. The boar took seventeen in the face and plowed a furrow to a stop three feet from my boots. The wolves came apart in the air. The stalker fell into a rising wall of them and hit the ground beside the boar, leaking from twenty-odd holes.

Four kills in three seconds. The needles dissolved back into the armor, and Cluster Six topped up the barrier without being asked.

Level 11 → 13.

Shiryeon looked at the corpses lying in a rough ring around me. "Omnidirectional deployment with self-guiding fins," she murmured, more to herself than to me, cataloguing it. "Twelve seconds ago you were wasting energy on goblins. This is better."

I wasn't finished. I fed Cluster Six a second order, sustain these, and grew six tendrils from the ridge of my spine, each seven feet long, thick at the base and tapering to a point. They unfurled behind me like the limbs of something from the deep sea, black and smooth with violet light running along them. I didn't have to steer all six. Clusters Two through Five split the motor control between them. I supplied the intent, and they carried it out. One coiled around a tree trunk and squeezed until the bark split. Another drove clean through the dead boar and slid back out.

Shiryeon had stopped walking. Her hand rested on her sword hilt. It was instinct, not a decision.

"You just grew six extra limbs," she said carefully. "Passively sustained." She watched them sway. "You're not human anymore. You understand that."

It wasn't a question, and I didn't answer it.

Something roared deep in the Glade, and two more roars answered it. Trees cracked and fell, coming closer. Shiryeon drew her blade, and a thin smile crossed her lips.

"Something big heard your invitation. Finally."

Three rock trolls came through the collapsing tree line, each twelve feet tall, with stone-grey skin and tiny burning orange eyes. They hunted as a pack.

I sharpened the tips of my tendrils to an edge only intent could hold and charged straight in.

The first troll's fist came down like a boulder. I slid under it, and two tendrils speared up, one through its wrist and one through its throat. The second troll tried to crush me between its arms, so I wrapped one arm, wrenched it until the joint cracked like bedrock, and drove a third tendril through its eye socket and out the back of its skull. It fell like a tree. All six closed on the first troll from six angles at once, and it went still. The third turned to run. A single tendril crossed thirty feet like a javelin and severed its spine between the shoulder blades, and two more finished it in the dirt.

Four seconds for three trolls.

Level 13 → 16.

Shiryeon stepped over a severed troll hand. She hadn't swung her blade once. "Four seconds," she said. "Three level fourteens." She looked toward the deeper Glade, where the trees grew darker and the sounds lower and older. "Two days ago you couldn't throw a punch without breaking your wrist. You're ready for the deep Glade. They set traps there. They ambush. They learn." A glance back at me. "Four more levels, Apostle."

I stopped first and turned my attention inward. I'm not reckless about systems I'm still running. Integration sat at ninety-seven percent, exactly where it had been when I woke. The last three percent was concentrated in my deep brain tissue, my inner ear, and my optic nerves, delicate structures the clusters were converting slowly so they wouldn't damage them.

Clever. The clusters had learned that on their own.

"Integration ninety-seven percent," I said. "Done within hours. They're being careful with the sensory tissue."

"Good," Shiryeon said, stepping over the threshold into the twilight under the ancient trees. "Because what lives in here won't give you time to recalibrate."

The air under that canopy was heavy and wet, and my tendrils felt them before I saw them: many signatures, spread wide and high, all around us.

"Canopy pack," Shiryeon said, low. "Twelve, minimum. Welcome to the deep Glade."

Something in me lit up like a struck match.

I let it all out. The exoskeleton thickened, layer compressing onto layer with audible clicks. The chest broadened, ridges rose along the shoulders, and greaves locked around my shins. Dark matter crawled up my neck and over my skull and shaped itself: an angular jaw guard, narrow eye slits glowing violet from within, and two horns sweeping back from the temples. The helm sealed with a hiss, and my vision sharpened as the clusters in my optic nerves fed the data straight in. Then the blades came, two curved arm blades of dark matter running two and a half feet past my fists, their edges compressed to a molecule's width, humming as Cluster Six kept them fed.

"Battle Mode," I told nobody in particular. It deserved a name.

Shiryeon turned and looked at all of it, horns to blades to the six tendrils swaying at my back, for five full seconds. Her grip tightened on her sword, and it wasn't fear.

"Terrifying," she said quietly. It sounded almost like a compliment.

Then the canopy exploded.

They were shadow apes, level sixteen: four-armed, black-furred, silver-eyed, with serrated bone along their forearms. Six dropped in staggered waves, timed so a single blast couldn't catch them all, while four flanked through the undergrowth and two hung back high in the branches as spotters. They had watched the trolls die, and they had adapted.

I charged.

My right blade cut the first one in half on its way down. Four tendrils took the next two, one speared through and one torn apart at the shoulders. The second wave ricocheted off tree trunks, changing angle in midair to throw off my aim. It was smart, but not smart enough. The clusters tracked all six trajectories at once and fed me speed, mass, and intercept points, and I spun with both blades out and opened two of them across the middle. The flankers came in low, sixteen arms swinging bone at my legs. Tendrils hammered down and pinned two through the spine. A third was flung sideways into a trunk, and the fourth lunged for my helm and met my left blade through its open mouth. The spotters fled. One tendril caught the first at forty feet. A second pinned the other to the trunk it was climbing.

Twelve shadow apes in seven seconds.

Level 16 → 21.

I stood in the wreckage and laughed inside the helm. "And I'm not even done with my augmentations yet."

Shiryeon walked the battlefield the way she had at the Behemoth, reading each kill. She crouched beside the ape pinned to the tree and studied the wound. "Clean penetration through four inches of muscle and bone at forty feet," she said, wiping her fingers on her thigh, "while engaging nine other targets." When she turned to me, her amber eyes weren't measuring anymore. They looked almost like respect. "You've passed the recommended level."

Far off in the dark, something enormous shifted with a sound like grinding tectonic plates.

"We could go deeper. Higher-level prey." She paused. "Or we save your strength for Harren. Six hours through the southern edge. Your call. More power, or the mission?"

I let Battle Mode go. The helm peeled back, the blades withdrew, and the tendrils settled into a faint idle, barely visible against my back.

"We can always come back," I said. "His Majesty entrusted this mission to me. I'll see it done first. Then I'll get back to playing."

"Discipline over hunger." She gave a small nod and turned south. "Good. Most would have kept killing."

The trees thinned and the light changed. It wasn't the gold of open country, more a muted grey-green. Hours went by, and Shiryeon never once hesitated at a fork.

"Harren sits at the foot of the Greywash River," she said eventually. "A village. Three hundred civilians, maybe. The Empire turned it into a forward depot for their western garrison two months ago: grain, weapons, medical supplies, siege equipment. Everything bound for the front passes through it." She ducked a low branch. "Our newest scouting puts the garrison closer to two hundred than forty. Forty of those are mages. The rest are infantry and logistics. There's a captain. I've never seen him."

"To be clear," I said, "I'm only to destroy its ability to operate as a depot?"

She slowed slightly. "His Majesty's orders are specific. Destroy the depot. Eliminate the garrison's ability to function as a supply hub for the western front. The village and the civilians, the farmers and families, are irrelevant to the objective." She looked back at me. "He didn't say to spare them, either. He said how is yours to decide. If civilians die as collateral, he won't care. If you go out of your way to slaughter them for no tactical reason, he'll consider it wasteful. His Majesty despises waste."

The sound of running water grew louder.

"Once Harren can't supply the western garrison, their front line starves inside two weeks. No food, no replacement weapons, no medicine. Thousands of soldiers slowly losing the ability to fight." She let that settle. "One village depot. A cascading strategic collapse."

She stopped at the edge of a ridge and crouched, motioning me down beside her.

"There."

Below, in a shallow valley beside a wide, slow river, sat Harren: timber and stone houses with smoke rising from their chimneys. Beyond the village center, unmistakable, were rows of military tents, wooden warehouses, supply wagons lined along the riverbank, and soldiers walking patrols in pairs.

"Study it," Shiryeon said. "Take your time. Then tell me your plan."

I studied it. Physics students learn early that the most elegant solution usually belongs to whoever puts the most energy into the smallest point.

"Raw force," I said. "At nightfall. Until then, I meditate and build up the barrier and the armor."

I sat cross-legged in the shadow of the trees while Harren went about its afternoon below us, unaware.

I worked on the barrier first. I layered compression into Cluster Six's architecture, then had Cluster Two strike it from the inside as a controlled stress test. The barrier took the blow and spread the force across its whole surface. I compressed another layer, then another, adding density without adding drain. After an hour it was three times as dense as it had been that morning.

Then the armor. Clusters Three and Four began pre-forming the Battle Mode template under my skin, the helm, the plates, the greaves, and the blade channels, dormant and stacked just beneath the surface, ready to deploy in under a second instead of costing me precious seconds mid-fight.

Shiryeon sat with her back against a tree and watched the valley, tracking patrols and counting heads.

"Shift change is at sundown," she said quietly, without looking at me. "There's a ten-minute gap. Half the patrols are walking back, and the replacements aren't in position yet."

She offered it the way you'd hand someone a blade, hilt first.

The sun crawled west, and inside me the clusters finished their work.

One hundred percent.

The last pocket of human biology dissolved. For a moment everything went silent and dark, as my optic nerves rebuilt and my inner ear recalibrated. Then sensation flooded back sharper than ever. The colors were vivid enough to hurt. From the ridge, five hundred yards away, I could hear individual conversations in Harren. A soldier was complaining about his boots. Someone was laughing at a joke I'd missed.

I was no longer human in any biological sense. Every cell, fiber, and neuron was mana-blood tissue built on a dark-matter frame.

The sun touched the western mountains. Shiryeon stood.

"Twenty minutes to sundown. The gap opens when the last patrol turns back toward the barracks."

Down below, torches were being lit along the perimeter, and the depot glowed orange in the dusk. Somebody was cooking meat. The river caught the last of the light and turned it to copper.

Two hundred soldiers, forty mages, and one captain. None of them had any idea what was sitting on the ridge above them.

Darkness came.
