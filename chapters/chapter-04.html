Chapter 4

The Inner Ring

3,263 words · about 14 min

‹ Interlude: The Empty Seat Contents Chapter 5 ›

I woke feeling different.

Yesterday's soreness had mostly faded. The frost plums had done their work, and my mana was full, the cold core humming quietly behind my ribs. I ate the rest of the jerky and ember berries and felt warmth spread down my arms and legs.

When Shiryeon knocked, she was already armed and ready, daggers on her hips and her black leather freshly oiled. Her white hair was pulled back tight, which made the long scar across her nose and cheek stand out even more.

She looked me up and down.

"You look less pathetic than yesterday," she said. "That's progress."

On the way out she gave me the rules. "The inner ring holds creatures between fifteen and twenty-five. At seven, everything in there outclasses you. The only reasons I'm taking you are that His Majesty ordered it and your fighting style makes up for the level gap." She glanced back. "But understand this. One mistake in there and you die. I won't always be fast enough to save you."

We came out under the red morning sky. The tree line of the Monster Glade waited on the horizon.

"Ready, Apostle?"

"Almost," I said. "Can we pick up supplies first? Mana potions, concealment tools, anything useful?"

She paused mid-stride. "Hm. Not a bad thought."

The quartermaster's stockroom was down a spiral stair lit by green-flame torches, behind a heavy, iron-bound door. Inside, shelves and crates lined every wall: vials, pouches, blades, snares. Behind the long counter sat a gaunt, grey-green creature with four arms, small round spectacles perched on a beak of a nose, and a bald head covered in faint ritual tattoos. Two of his hands were writing in a ledger while the other two sorted a crate of vials.

"Ottrak," Shiryeon said.

All four hands stopped at once.

"Commander." His voice sounded like dry parchment. "And ah... the Apostle. I've heard about you." His small yellow eyes studied me with clinical interest. "What do you need?"

Two arms swept out across the room. "Minor mana potions, smoke pellets, scent-masking salve, tripwire snares, flash powder. All available to field operatives. Supply isn't infinite, though. One standard reconnaissance loadout per sortie." He pushed his spectacles up his beak. "Choose wisely."

"Mana potions and the scent-masking salve," I said. "Efficiency is everything on a hunt."

"Practical." He set three small glass vials on the counter, each holding a swirling deep-blue liquid that pulsed faintly. "Each restores about a hundred mana. Don't drink more than one in a short span. Your body needs time to absorb it, or the excess destabilizes." A pause. "Unpleasant results."

He added a small clay jar sealed with wax.

"Neck, wrists, ankles. It lasts about four hours. Most things in the Glade hunt by smell first and sight second. This makes you invisible to their noses." He slid the ledger toward me. "Sign."

Above my line were the names of soldiers, scouts, and generals. I signed Mephesto under them, and he inspected the signature, nodded, and filed it away.

Outside the gates Shiryeon didn't slow down. "Apply the salve now. We'll be in the inner ring in twenty minutes."

The salve was cold, grey, and odorless, and it sank into the skin almost at once. A strange numbness followed, as if my scent had been erased. I couldn't even smell myself.

"Understood, Shiryeon," I said, smearing the last of it on my ankles. "Let us proceed into the fray."

She didn't dignify that with an answer.

We crossed the outer ring quickly. Shiryeon moved through the trees like a ghost and never disturbed a branch. Exactly twenty minutes later, I felt the change.

The trees grew larger, then enormously larger, until their trunks were as wide as buildings and the canopy closed overhead so tightly that almost no light reached the ground. The air thickened, and a low vibration ran through the earth beneath my boots, as if something vast were breathing far below.

"Welcome to the inner ring," Shiryeon said quietly.

The outer ring had been constant noise: chittering, rustling, distant roars. Here there was nothing at all.

That was worse.

She drew one dagger slowly. "Something's already watching us. Sixty meters, northeast, in the canopy."

I looked up. Two enormous pale eyes stared down from the branches, each the size of a dinner plate, unblinking.

"Shadowstalker," she breathed. "Level eighteen. Fast, silent. An ambush predator."

The eyes shifted. It was moving.

"Your call, Apostle."

It hunted by motion. That was the obvious lever. I reached for the core and tried to shape a copy of myself, a decoy that would run.

The energy resisted. At level seven, a true clone was far beyond me. But a rough silhouette, a moving shape, I could manage. I pushed dark matter out through my palm and along the ground, and a vague humanoid figure rose from the forest floor. It was barely three feet tall and flickering badly. Up close it wouldn't fool anything. In this darkness, against a predator that tracked movement, it might.

I sent it running to the left.

The canopy exploded.

The Shadowstalker dropped like a black comet: six legs, sleek obsidian fur, a flat skull crowded with needle-thin teeth, long limbs ending in razor tips. From nose to tail it was easily twelve feet, a body built for killing, and its huge pale eyes were locked on the running shape.

It hit the decoy at full speed. I closed my fist.

The decoy detonated in a violent burst of dark matter. The Shadowstalker shrieked, a high, horrible sound that shook leaves loose overhead, as the blast tore open its left side and sprayed black blood across the roots.

It didn't die. It rolled, came up, and turned its head toward me, and those pale eyes narrowed. It knows.

It lunged, impossibly fast.

Shiryeon was suddenly between us in a blur, one dagger turning a massive claw aside in a shower of sparks. "Finish it!"

Its wounded flank was open, ribs showing, dark blood pouring.

Needles wouldn't do it. It was too big and too fast, and every pinhole I made would be the last before it reached me. I needed to hold it. So I thrust both hands forward and let the dark matter out as filaments instead of points: black threads that whipped through the air like living wire.

One wrapped around its throat. Another coiled around its middle, right where the wound gaped. A third snared its hind legs, and a fourth caught a forelimb mid-swipe and dragged it back. The beast thrashed, and its jaws snapped inches from Shiryeon's face. She ducked and rolled clear.

I clenched both fists and pulled.

The strands bit. Obsidian fur split where they cut in. The thread around its middle sank into the blast wound and found the exposed ribs, and bone cracked. My mana drained fast enough that I could feel the core dimming, but the strands held. Then the one around its throat cut through.

The sound was wet and terrible. Its head came away, its body convulsed, and the remaining strands sliced through together. Pieces of the Shadowstalker hit the forest floor in heavy thuds.

I lowered my hands. My arms were shaking, and sweat dripped from my jaw.

Shiryeon stood a few feet away with both daggers drawn. "Messy," she said at last. "But effective."

Shadowstalker (Lv. 18) slain. Level 7 → 9.

She nudged a severed limb with her boot. She could feel the shift in me, the way she always seemed to. "Level nine already. You're taking in power faster than anything I've seen in two hundred years." Her head tilted. "And that scream will draw attention. Scavengers. Hunters."

As if she'd summoned it, a deep, guttural rumble rolled through the trees from the south, and the ground trembled faintly under our feet.

"Something big," she murmured. "Coming this way. Maybe five minutes." Her amber eyes cut to me. "Drink a potion and recover, or push on while you have the momentum. Your call, Apostle. Decide fast."

I uncorked a potion and drank it. It went down ice-cold and burst outward through my veins like blue lightning, and the core flared, drinking greedily. Then I turned south.

"Let them come," I said. "I've only just begun to taste this power."

It came out of the trees like a landslide.

It was the size of a house. Four legs like stone pillars carried a body armored in overlapping plates of bone-white shell. Six small red eyes sat in two rows across a massive horned skull, and its open mouth showed rows of flat grinding teeth stained with old blood. A tail lined with jagged spikes dragged behind it. Steam poured from its nostrils.

"Ironhide Behemoth," Shiryeon said, low and tight, from behind me. "Level twenty-two. Very high defense. Those plates deflect most physical and magical attacks. Weak points are the joints between the plates, and the underbelly."

It lowered its skull and pawed the earth. Then it charged.

The ground shook with every step, and trees splintered out of its path. Sixty meters. Forty. Twenty.

I had no way to stop that much mass head-on, so I didn't try. I ran a thin coat of dark matter down my legs, a layer of crackling black too sheer to count as armor but enough for one burst, and jumped.

It launched me up and to the right just as the Behemoth tore through the spot where I'd stood. The wind of its passing rattled my teeth. I caught a thick branch, swung myself higher, and scrambled up the trunk.

From above I could see what I needed. The bone plates covered its back, sides, and skull like interlocking shields, almost seamless. But where the front legs met the torso, dark grey skin showed through gaps the plates left so the beast could move. There was another soft patch under the belly, between the rear legs.

The Behemoth skidded to a halt, gouging the earth. Its six eyes swept the canopy and found me. It roared, and the sound was a physical blow. Bark peeled off the trunk under my hands, and my vision smeared.

Then it rammed its horned skull into the base of my tree.

The trunk cracked through, and the world tilted. The tree began to fall with me on it.

Shiryeon came in from the flank in a silver blur and slashed across the joint of its left foreleg. The Behemoth bellowed and lurched sideways.

I was falling, and the exposed joint of its right foreleg was directly below me.

Everything slowed. I stretched out my right hand and poured a hundred points of mana into a single point, a needle no bigger than a finger. I kept compressing it, harder and harder, until it was no longer black but a deep, violent purple that vibrated with barely contained force.

I threw it.

It punched through the soft grey skin as if it were paper, buried itself six inches deep, and detonated inside the joint.

There was a muffled crack from within the creature. Its right foreleg folded, and dark red blood fountained from the wound as it staggered with an agonized bellow that shook the canopy.

I hit the ground hard and rolled through dirt and roots. My shoulder took the impact in a white flash of pain. Then I was up, alive, and nearly empty.

The Behemoth dragged its shattered leg and swung its skull toward me, all six eyes blazing. Shiryeon struck again from behind and drove both daggers into its left rear joint. It kicked back savagely, caught her across the middle, and threw her into a tree trunk with a sickening crack. She rolled to her feet at once and spat blood.

"The belly!" she shouted. "It's stumbling! Get underneath!"

It wobbled on two ruined legs, and its exposed underside sagged low.

I didn't have the mana for another heavy needle. But I'd spent a semester on statistics, and the principle is the same: when one big thing can't do the job, use a great many small ones.

I thrust both hands up and let the dark matter pour out with no precision at all, only volume. Dozens of hair-thin needles went up in a rapid-fire storm, each costing a sliver of mana, together draining me fast. One, five, ten, twenty. They slammed into the soft grey flesh like a hailstorm of black thorns. The Behemoth screamed and tried to rear back, but its ruined legs betrayed it and it pitched forward instead, belly dropping lower still. Thirty. Forty. Its underside bristled with buried needles, each pulsing faintly.

I closed both fists.

"Detonate."

The explosion tore it apart from the inside. Bone plates cracked and flew outward like shrapnel. One massive horn spun end over end and embedded itself in a tree. The ground beneath the beast cratered, and what was left of it came down in two halves with a wet, final thud.

Silence. Blood dripped from the canopy, and steam rose from the remains. I stood in the middle of it, shaking, covered in blood that wasn't mine.

Ironhide Behemoth (Lv. 22) slain. Level 9 → 11.

Shiryeon limped over, wiping blood from her mouth, and stared at the devastation.

"His Majesty chose well," she said quietly.

"Save the praise, Shiryeon." I was already scanning the trees. "We're wounded, and the scent of this kill will draw others. Check your gear. We move."

She stared at me. Something shifted behind her amber eyes. It wasn't offense. It was closer to approval.

"Giving me orders now," she said flatly. But she checked her daggers, rolled her shoulder, and winced, pressing a hand to her ribs. "Two cracked. Manageable."

Somewhere above the canopy, wings were beating wetly. Clicking and rustling were coming closer. Scavengers.

"There's a dead zone ten minutes from here," she said, already moving and favoring her left side. "Rock formation. Nothing nests there. We rest and reassess."

The dead zone was a cluster of grey boulders forming a natural alcove, with no tracks, no nests, and no sound. Shiryeon dropped against a rock and let out a slow breath. Behind us, the first scavengers had reached the Behemoth, and the tearing carried through the trees.

"Drink a potion," she said. "We aren't done yet."

I had two left, and Ottrak's warning in my head. I drank them both.

The first hit like a cold wave. The second followed it straight in, and my veins burned and my stomach clenched. A sharp spike of pain went through my chest, and the core flickered erratically. The world swam, and nausea rolled up my throat.

Then it passed and settled. The mana was mine, but my hands shook and a dull ache stayed lodged behind my eyes. Unpleasant results. Noted.

Shiryeon watched me drink both and said nothing. Her face said plenty.

I drew dark matter thinly across my whole body. It wasn't armor. It was a diffuse layer that seeped outward like smoke and bent light and shadow around us. At level eleven it was only a partial distortion, not invisibility, but in the gloom of the inner ring it was close enough.

"Move," I said.

We pushed northeast through thinning forest, the vast trunks giving way to ordinary trees and the oppressive weight of the inner ring lifting by degrees. Thirty minutes later we were back among the familiar chittering of the outer ring, where nothing could threaten me anymore.

Shiryeon leaned against a tree and pressed her ribs. "Two kills. Level eleven. Half a day." She looked at me sideways. "His Majesty wanted level ten by the end of the week. You've passed it already."

I looked at her. She was standing very straight, a little too straight.

"Enough about me," I said. "How are you feeling?"

She blinked. For a fraction of a second genuine surprise crossed her scarred face before she smothered it.

"Functional."

She shifted against the trunk and winced. The leather over her left side was dented inward where the Behemoth's kick had landed, and a thin line of blood seeped between her fingers. It was dark purple, not red. I filed that away.

"Two cracked ribs. Some internal bruising. Nothing that won't heal in a few days." She paused. "Demon bodies recover faster than human ones." Her amber eyes narrowed, studying me as if I were a puzzle. "Why do you ask?"

I didn't have a good answer, so I didn't give one.

She pushed off the tree and stood at full height, pain and all. Two hundred years of soldiering wouldn't let her show weakness. "Don't mistake me for something fragile, Apostle. I've fought with worse than this. The castle has a bone-mender, Old Corvane. I'll see him when we get back."

"Good to know." I took a step toward her. "Can I carry you the rest of the way? I can set your gravity to zero. You'd be weightless."

She stopped. Then she turned, slowly, and looked at me with eyes that could have cut glass.

"No."

It landed like a blade on stone.

"I have walked back to this castle missing an arm, Apostle. I have crawled back with both legs shattered. I once dragged myself through mud and blood for three days with a poisoned spear in my spine." Cracked ribs and all, she stepped toward me, towering and dangerous. "I do not get carried."

She turned and walked. Her stride was even, every step controlled. She refused to limp.

I followed in silence for forty minutes.

At the castle gate two armored ogres snapped to attention at the sight of her, and their eyes slid curiously to me. She passed them without a glance. Inside, she finally stopped and turned.

"War room. Ten minutes. Clean yourself up first." She looked at my clothes, which had stiffened with dried blood. "You look like a butcher's apprentice."

Then she walked off toward the medical wing, one hand still pressed to her side.

"Yes, Commander," I said to her back, and went.

Someone had refilled the basin in my room while I was gone. I suspected Bris. I stripped off the ruined clothes and scrubbed until the water ran black-red. Cuts and bruises covered my arms and chest, and my shoulder was turning a deep purple where I'd landed on it. A fresh set of dark clothes waited folded on the bed: a simple black tunic, trousers, and boots. Someone in this castle anticipated my needs very well.

Level eleven in a day and a half. I pulled up the window, just to look at it.

Two days ago my attack had been fifteen. I closed the window and went.

Two turns, one staircase up, and down a hall of green-flame torches. The war room doors were black iron etched with symbols I couldn't read, and two skeleton guards flanked them. Inside, a long stone table was covered in maps and carved markers, with candles burning in iron sconces along the walls.

The Demon Lord stood at the far end with his back to me, studying a great map pinned to the wall. The candlelight caught his golden hair and the curve of his yellow horns.

He spoke without turning.

"Level eleven. Impressive."

"It will only continue to rise, my liege," I said, and bowed.
